I.          BACKGROUND

            A.     Factual Background

            HireRight is a consumer reporting agency (“CRA”) subject to the Fair Credit Reporting

Act (“FCRA”), 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681 et seq. See ECF No. 1 ¶ 19. As a CRA, HireRight provides

employment screening reports, which include an applicant’s criminal history. Id. ¶ 1. The

FRCA requires CRAs to follow “reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy

of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates.” 15 U.S.C. §

1681e(b).

       In December 2021, Plaintiff applied for a job with three trucking companies: Big G

Express, Inc. (“Big G”), Magnum LTD (“Magnum”), and Ceva Logistics US, Inc. (“Ceva”).

were inaccurate and outdated in violation of the FCRA. See id. ¶ 11. He alleges information

regarding his criminal records was publicly available and easily verifiable, but HireRight did

not have a defined process in place to verify the accuracy of the information it provided to his

prospective employers. Id. ¶¶ 12–13.

       B.      Procedural History

       Plaintiff sued HireRight on December 18, 2023, asserting two causes of action under

the FCRA. First, he alleges HireRight violated 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) by failing to follow

reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the information in the reports.

See ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 27–32. Second, he claims a violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1681k(a)(2) for

HireRight’s alleged failure to maintain strict procedures designed to ensure that the reports

were complete and up to date. Id. ¶¶ 33–38.

II.      LEGAL STANDARD

         A.     Summary Judgment

         Summary judgment is appropriate if a party “shows that there is no genuine dispute as

to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P.

56(a). “In determining whether a genuine issue has been raised, the court must construe all

inferences and ambiguities in favor of the nonmoving party.” HealthSouth Rehab. Hosp. v. Am.

Nat’l Red Cross, 101 F.3d 1005, 1008 (4th Cir. 1996). The party seeking summary judgment

shoulders the initial burden of proving to the court that there is no genuine dispute of material

fact. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). A fact is “material” if it “might affect

the outcome of the suit under the governing law.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S.

242, 248 (1986). A dispute of material fact is “genuine” “if the evidence is such that a

reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party.” Id. A party asserting that a

fact is genuinely disputed must support the assertion by “citing to particular parts of materials

in the record, including depositions, documents, electronically stored information, affidavits

or declarations, stipulations (including those made for purposes of the motion only),

admissions, interrogatory answers, or other materials.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(A).

       B.      15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b)

       Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b), “[w]henever a consumer reporting agency prepares a

consumer report it shall follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy

of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates.” A CRA violates

this statute if “(1) the consumer report contains inaccurate information and (2) the reporting

agency did not follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy.” Dalton

v. Cap. Associated Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d 409, 415 (4th Cir. 2001).

       A CRA may rely on the accuracy of information obtained from a reputable source it

reasonably believes to be trustworthy if the information appears plausible and credible on its

face and the CRA lacks notice of any problems regarding the source. See Letren v. Trans Union,

LLC, No. PX 15-3361, 2017 WL 445237, at **8–9 (D. Md. Feb. 2, 2017). “Requiring credit

reporting agencies to look beyond the face of every court document to find the rare case when

a document incorrectly reports the results of the underlying action would be unduly

burdensome and inefficient in the absence of a consumer dispute.” Id. at *9 (citation modified).

       The “FCRA imposes liability for negligent noncompliance with the Act, and it allows

for enhanced penalties for willful violations.” Dalton, 257 F.3d at 417. A CRA willfully violates

the Act if it “knowingly and intentionally commit[s] an act in conscious disregard for the rights

of the consumer.” Id. at 418 (citations and quotations omitted). When the alleged violation is

only negligent, the plaintiff must show actual damages—i.e., that the inclusion of the

inaccurate information caused him injury. See 15 U.S.C. § 1681o; Spitzer v. Trans Union LLC,

140 F. Supp. 2d 562, 564 (E.D.N.C. 2000).

III.   DISCUSSION

       Defendant urges the court to grant summary judgment for three reasons. First,

Defendant argues that Plaintiff lacks admissible evidence that HireRight’s reports caused him

actual damages. See ECF No. 60-20 at 6–9. As a result, Defendant contends it is entitled to

summary judgment on Plaintiff’s claim for negligent violation of the statute. See id. Second,

Defendant maintains there is no evidence that HireRight acted recklessly, as would support a

claim for willful violation of the statute. Id. at 9–11. Third, Defendant argues that Plaintiff

cannot support his claim for punitive damages. Id. at 11–13.

                2.     Emotional Damages

         A plaintiff suing for negligent violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) may also satisfy the

“actual damages” requirement by showing that the inaccurate report caused him emotional

distress. See, e.g., Dalton, 257 F.3d at 418–19. Claims of emotional distress, however, are

often “fraught with vagueness and speculation,” and “easily susceptible to fictitious and trivial

claims.” Sloane v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC, 510 F.3d 495, 503 (4th Cir. 2007) (quoting Price

v. City of Charlotte, 93 F.3d 1241, 1250 (4th Cir. 1996)). “For this reason, although specifically

recognizing that a plaintiff’s testimony can provide sufficient evidence to support an emotional

distress award, the Fourth Circuit has required a plaintiff to reasonably and sufficiently explain

the circumstances of the injury and not resort to mere conclusory statements.” Id. (citation

modified). Courts look to the following factors when considering a claim for emotional

distress:

         the factual context in which the emotional distress arose; evidence corroborating
         the testimony of the plaintiff; the nexus between the conduct of the defendant
         and the emotional distress; the degree of such mental distress; mitigating
         circumstances, if any; physical injuries suffered due to the emotional distress;
         medical attention resulting from the emotional duress; psychiatric or
         psychological treatment; and the loss of income, if any.

Id.

               1.         Plaintiff’s Challenges to HireRight’s Procedures

       The South Carolina Department of Probation, Parole, and Pardon Services (the

“SCDPPPS”) processes pardon applications and makes pardon information available to the

public on its website. See ECF No. 64 at 8. When the SCDPPPS issues a pardon, it mails

certified copies of the pardon certificate to the South Carolina clerk of court where the

conviction occurred so that the county court can update its records. See id. The SCDPPPS

mailed copies of Plaintiff’s pardon certificate to the Marion County Clerk of Court after his

hearing on December 1, 2021. [ECF No. 60-4.]

       When preparing the reports at issue, HireRight searched the Marion County Public

Index for any records pertaining to Plaintiff. [ECF Nos. 60-20 at 3.] But because the Marion

County Clerk of Court had yet to receive Plaintiff’s pardon certificate from SCDPPPS, his

pardon was not reflected on the public index when HireRight performed its searches on

December 17, December 21, and December 28, 2021. Id. As a result, HireRight did not report

Plaintiff’s pardon. Id.

                      iv.     Failure to Search the SLED Database

       Finally, Plaintiff argues HireRight could have avoided the omission by paying for a

“economic disaster,” and “CRAs need not engage in economically infeasible investigations to

operate.” Id. (citing Jianqing Wu v. Trans Union, No. AW-03-1290, 2006 WL 4729755, at *7

       The record contains no evidence from which a jury could find that HireRight acted with

reckless disregard of Plaintiff’s rights. Defendant is therefore entitled to summary judgment

on Plaintiff’s claim for willful violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) and his corresponding request

for punitive damages is denied.

IV.    CONCLUSION

       Plaintiff has not produced evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that he

suffered actual damages due to HireRight’s conduct, nor has he produced sufficient evidence

to suggest that HireRight acted with reckless disregard for his rights. As a result, the court

GRANTS Defendant’s motion for summary judgment.
