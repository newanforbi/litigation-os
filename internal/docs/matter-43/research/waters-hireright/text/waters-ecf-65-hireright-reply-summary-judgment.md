II.      ARGUMENT

         A.     HireRight Did Not Cause Plaintiff Actual Damages

                1. Plaintiff Lacks Admissible Evidence That HireRight Caused Him Economic
                   Harm.

         HireRight is entitled to summary judgment on Plaintiff’s negligence claim seeking

economic damages because he lacks any evidence to support it. As HireRight explained in its

Motion, Plaintiff has introduced no evidence that any employer or prospective employer denied

him a job because HireRight did not report his pardon. (See Motion, ECF No. 60 at 6-7).

         In his Opposition, the best Plaintiff can do is cite inadmissible, hearsay. Specifically, he

argues that a Big G employee (Blake Smith) supposedly told him the reason Big G declined him

employment. (Pltf. Opp., ECF No. 64 at 9). HireRight need not belabor the point: Plaintiff’s

testimony about what Blake Smith allegedly told him is textbook hearsay and inadmissible under

Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c). If Plaintiff wanted to use statements from Blake Smith, he needed

to depose him. See Myrick v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC, No. 5:15-CV-00562-BR, 2017 WL

99:22, 101:6-103:9, 110:13-20).

       Because Plaintiff offers no admissible evidence to support his claim that Big G (or any

explained that it vetted the Marion County Public Index, has substantial experience with it, and

64 at 4, 5, 12-14). He is wrong. A CRA does not “willfully” violate the FCRA if it does not search

every conceivable source (like the SCDPPPS website) for a record that may possibly exist. Letren

       Plaintiff cites one case – Wilson v. CoreLogic SafeRent, LLC, No. 14-CV-2477 (JPO), 2017

       First, Wilson didn’t even address whether the defendant’s conduct constituted a willful

violation of the FCRA. Wilson, 2017 WL 4357568, at *3 n.2 (explicitly noting that “SafeRent d[id]

the case is completely irrelevant. The issue before this Court is whether Plaintiff has introduced

evidence that HireRight acted willfully, and such reckless disregard of his rights, when it searched

a source that it reasonably believed would include pardon information.

       Second, Wilson actually supports HireRight’s position. In Wilson, SafeRent (a CRA)

conducted a search of its own proprietary “Multistate Database” for criminal records information

(not a public index or court website). Id. at *1. That database contained information from various

       Plaintiff’s misunderstanding aside, it is not “reckless” for a CRA to rely on court websites

that maintain disclaimers. In fact, courts have consistently held that CRAs may rely on court

websites with disclaimers. In Fraase, for example, a CRA searched for criminal information on

the North Dakota Courts website, which included a disclaimer that it was “without warranty of

any kind.” Fraase v. Advantage Credit Bureau, No. 3:23-cv-117, 2025 WL 947546, at *1 (D.N.D.

Mar. 28, 2025). As it turns out, a municipal court grouped two brothers in the same case “jacket,”

and the CRA reported a record as belonging to the plaintiff when it was his brother’s. Id. Because

the CRA believed the court website was reliable and the plaintiff had only the website’s disclaimer

and the error on his own report to show it was not, the district court held the CRA used reasonable

procedures and granted summary judgment on plaintiff’s § 1681e(b) claim. Id. at *5.6

       HireRight’s case is even stronger than the CRA’s in Fraase for two reasons. First,

               4. The FCRA Does Not Require CRAs to Search Both the County Court Record
                  and the State Law Enforcement Database (“SLED”).

       Lastly, Plaintiff asserts HireRight “could have improved the accuracy of Plaintiff’s Report”

by ordering a SLED report in his statement of facts section but does not even apply it in his legal

argument. (See Pltf. Opp., ECF No. 64 at 7, 12-15). Nonetheless, this argument fails for the same

reason his SCDPPPS argument fails: the FCRA does not require CRAs to search every conceivable

source (like SLED) for information about a record, particularly when a CRA reasonably believes

the source it is searching is reliable and it lacks notice of systemic problems to the contrary. See

supra Sec. II.A. Without evidence to the contrary, Plaintiff cannot establish HireRight’s actions

created “an unjustifiably high risk of harm that is either known or so obvious that it should be

known” for a willful violation. Safeco, 551 U.S. at 68.

       Plaintiff’s position would also create an economic disaster. Courts have held that CRAs

need not engage in economically infeasible investigations to operate – what is required is that a

CRA rely on records it has identified as trustworthy absent evidence to the contrary. See Jianqing

Wu v. Trans Union, No. AW-03-1290, 2006 WL 4729755, at *6 (D. Md. May 2, 2006), aff’d, 219

F. App’x 320 (4th Cir. 2007) (“To make [a CRA] liable under the FCRA for relying on the record

of businesses, which [the CRA] has identified as trustworthy, would vitiate one of the purposes of

the FCRA, to facilitate commerce. Under the system Plaintiff proposes, the economic costs of

investigation would render it economically infeasible for CRAs to function. Nothing in the FCRA

or its implementing regulations evinces the intent to place such heavy burdens on CRAs.”); see

also, Letren, 2017 WL 445237, at *9 (“Requiring [CRAs] to look beyond the face of every court

document to find the rare case when a document incorrectly reports the result of the underlying

action would be unduly burdensome and inefficient[.]”) (quoting Henson, 29 F.3d at 285-86).
