                   CONCISE SUMMARY OF NATURE OF THE CASE
       In December 2021, HireRight prepared three background check reports about Plaintiff that

included his felony drug conviction. He claims the reports were inaccurate because HireRight did

not also report that he had been pardoned earlier that month. But the record lacks any admissible

evidence that HireRight’s reports caused him harm, that HireRight “willfully” violated the Fair

Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”), or that HireRight’s actions were so reprehensible that they justify

an award of punitive damages.

       Plaintiff has introduced no evidence—none—that any of the three prospective employers

denied him a job because HireRight did not report his pardon. In fact, the undisputed evidence

shows Plaintiff turned down offers from two employers for whom HireRight prepared a report

and stopped communicating with the third, so they never made a hiring decision.

       Likewise, Plaintiff offers no evidence that HireRight willfully violated the FCRA. As it

turns out, the online Marion County Public Index, where HireRight found his felony conviction,

did not reflect Plaintiff’s pardon because the court clerk had not yet received a copy in the mail

                                      LEGAL STANDARD

         Summary judgment must be granted when “the movant shows that there is no genuine

dispute as to any material fact.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). This can be done by either producing

affirmative “evidence that negates an essential element of the nonmoving party’s claim” or

showing that the nonmoving party’s evidence is “insufficient to establish an essential element” of

his claim at trial. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 331 (1986). “A district court must grant

summary judgment if, after an adequate time for discovery, a party fails to make a showing

sufficient to establish the existence of an essential element of that party’s case.” Baber v. Hosp.

Corp. of Am., 977 F.2d 872, 874 (4th Cir. 1992) (citing Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322).

                                           ARGUMENT

         HireRight is entitled to summary judgment because no genuine issue of material fact exists

on elements of Plaintiff’s claims. The undisputed facts show that HireRight’s reports did not cause

him actual damages and HireRight did not willfully violate the FCRA or commit any reprehensible

act that would justify punitive damages.

I.       The undisputed facts show Plaintiff did not suffer harm as a result of HireRight’s
         reports.

         To survive summary judgment, Plaintiff must offer evidence that he suffered “actual

damages . . . as a result of the failure” of HireRight to comply with the FCRA. 15 U.S.C. § 1681o;

see Spitzer v. Trans Union LLC, 140 F. Supp. 2d 562, 564 (E.D. N.C. 2000) (noting “negligent

noncompliance with § 1681e(b)” requires that “the consumer’s injury was caused by the inclusion

of the inaccurate entry”).35 Here, the record lacks any admissible evidence that HireRight’s report

caused Plaintiff actual damages.

       A.      Plaintiff received job offers from two employers and ghosted a third.
       At summary judgment, Plaintiff needs evidence, not speculation, that a prospective

employer rejected his job application because of HireRight’s reports to claim economic damages.

See, e.g., Davenport v. Sallie Mae, Inc., 124 F. Supp. 3d 574, 583 (D. Md. 2015) (Mem.) (granting

defendant summary judgment where plaintiff provided insufficient evidence that a credit report

affected a hiring decision). He has none.

       The undisputed facts show that Ceva and Magnum offered Plaintiff jobs and that he

rejected them. (Ex. 13, Pltf. Resp to Interrog. No. 6). The record also lacks any admissible evidence

that Big G denied him employment based on HireRight’s report. In fact, after telling Big G about

his pardon (Ex. 2, Pltf. Tr., 167:16–168:21), Plaintiff failed to complete the application process

(Ex. 14, J. Fairchild Tr., 60:16–61:1, 119:18–120:5). Thus, Big G never made a decision on his

has no evidence that HireRight’s reports cost him any job.

       B.      Plaintiff’s conclusory statements of emotional distress prove nothing.
       Like his claim for economic damages, Plaintiff lacks evidence sufficient to create a genuine

issue of material fact to support his claim for emotional distress. Because emotional distress “is

easily susceptible to fictitious and trivial claims,” the Fourth Circuit requires plaintiffs supporting

emotional distress solely with their own testimony to “reasonably and sufficiently explain the

circumstances of [his] injury and not resort to mere conclusory statements.” Price v. City of

Charlotte, 93 F.3d 1241, 1250 (4th Cir. 1996). Generic recitations of “depression” and feelings of

(4th Cir. 2002) (holding plaintiff did not establish emotional distress despite colorful testimony

symptoms, loss of income, and impact on the plaintiff’s conduct and lifestyle.” Chao, 306 F.3d at

503 (4th Cir. 2007). Whether a plaintiff’s emotional distress is “apparent to others” is also a

significant factor. Guthrie v. PHH Mortg. Corp., 79 F.4th 328, 346 (4th Cir. 2023) (citing Sloane,

from a plaintiff’s distress. See id. at 346–47.

II.    Plaintiff’s willfulness claim fails because the record is devoid of any evidence that
       HireRight recklessly violated § 1681e(b).

       The Court should enter summary judgment in favor of HireRight on Plaintiff’s claim that

it willfully violated the FCRA because no evidence supports a finding of willfulness.

       Section 1681e(b) requires CRAs to “follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum

possible accuracy of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates.” 15

U.S.C. § 1681e(b). Section 1681n penalizes a CRA “who willfully fails to comply with any

requirement” of the FCRA. 15 U.S.C. § 1681n(a). To prove a willful violation, Plaintiff must show

HireRight’s failure to report his pardon was reckless. Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr, 551 U.S. 47,

57–58 (2007); McIntyre v. RentGrow, Inc., 34 F.4th 87, 93 (1st Cir. 2022). Recklessness requires

acting with “an unjustifiably high risk of harm that is either known or so obvious that it should be

known.” Safeco, 551 U.S. at 68.

       Here, the record lacks any evidence that HireRight willfully violated the FCRA. To begin

with, case law provides that when a CRA relies on a source it believes is trustworthy and the CRA

has no notice of systemic problems with the source, the CRA has acted reasonably under

§ 1681e(b). Commentary on the Fair Credit Reporting Act, 16 C.F.R.§ Pt. 600, App.; Gadson v.

Experian Info. Sols, No. 2:23-cv-00029-BHH-MHC, 2024 WL 3745476, slip op. at *7 (D.S.C.

July 18, 2024); Sarver v. Experian Info. Sols, 390 F.3d 969, 972 (7th Cir. 2004).

       Courts also consistently hold it is presumptively reasonable for a CRA to rely on a court

docket, including an online docket. Dalton v. Cap. Associated Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d. 409, 417 (4th

       First, punitive damages can only follow a willfulness finding. See 15 U.S.C. § 1681n(a)(2).

Plaintiff has no evidence to support a willfulness finding. See supra Part II. HireRight had no

reason to know the Marion County Public Index would not include Plaintiff’s pardon. HireRight’s

reliance on the Public Index is “a far cry from the level of knowing and intelligent commission of

acts in conscious disregard for the rights of its customers.” Davenport, 124 F. Supp. 3d at 584

(dismissing punitive damages claim against furnisher that negligently reported loan as delinquent

despite where “it was apparent” its employees were not aware of what each other was doing and

its dispute process was “wanting”); cf. Daugherty v. Ocwen Loan Servicing, LLC, 701 F. App’x

246, 256 (4th Cir. 2017) (reducing punitive damages award where furnisher willfully violated the

FCRA by employing procedures that “systematically ignored” certain dispute codes of verification

requests containing more than one code).

       Second, conduct does not rise to the level of reprehensibility justifying punitive damages

where, for example, it (i) “involv[ed] an isolated instance of human error which . . . upon

discovery, [the CRA] quickly took ameliorative action” to fix, (ii) arose from the CRA’s

“reli[ance] on information it assumed was accurate,” or (iii) was based on its “assum[ption] [that]

its actions were lawful.” Saunders v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC, 469 F. Supp. 2d 343, 351 (E.D.

Va. 2007) (distinguishing the enumerated situations from conduct that would satisfy the

reprehensibility factor of State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell, 538 U.S. 408, 419 (2003)).

The touchstone of a punitive damages analysis is whether a punitive award would “punish[ ]

unlawful conduct and deter[ ] its repetition.” BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 568

(1996); see Yohay v. City of Alexandria Emps. Credit Union, Inc., 827 F.2d 967, 972 (4th Cir.

1987) (explaining punitive damages may be awarded only when the award “comports with the

underlying deterrent purpose of the FCRA”).

Gadson, 2024 WL 3745476, at *7; Sarver, 390 F.3d at 972. This case involved a single known

incident of a Public Index not reflecting a pardon when it was searched; HireRight did not

perpetuate policies it knew would lead to inaccuracies, turn a blind eye to problems, or ignore

similar issues from other consumers. See Saunders, 469 F. Supp. 2d at 351 (ongoing misconduct

factors in warranting punitive damages). On the contrary, HireRight had every reason to believe

its policies and procedures were designed to produce accurate reports. Because they did. (Ex. 16,

South Carolina Criminal and Offense History Search Accuracy Rate from June 2021 to June 2022,

HIRERIGHT_01095). Where Plaintiff’s pardon was not reported because of a timing issue,

punitive damages are not necessary here nor warranted to deter future wrongdoing.

       Because Plaintiff cannot maintain a claim for punitive damages, HireRight is entitled to

summary judgment on his punitive damages demand.

                                        CONCLUSION

       Plaintiff did not—and could not—gather facts to support his claims. On this record, he

cannot prove that HireRight’s report caused him damages or that HireRight acted willfully or

reprehensibly in preparing them. This absence of evidence is fatal to his case. There is nothing

here for a jury to decide. The Court should enter summary judgment in favor of HireRight.
