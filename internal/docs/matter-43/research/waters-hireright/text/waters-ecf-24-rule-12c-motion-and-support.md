Plaintiff Keith Eugene Waters has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. Fed.

about him were inaccurate and incomplete or outdated. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681e(b), 1681k(a). He has

docket is showing about Plaintiff’s criminal record—to this day. As a matter of law, HireRight’s

reports are correct, complete, and up-to-date. Therefore, the Court should grant HireRight’s

motion for judgment on the pleadings and enter judgment in HireRight’s favor on Plaintiff’s

claims.

        The Court should grant HireRight, LLC’s Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings

because HireRight did nothing wrong. Plaintiff’s case is premised on the theory that HireRight

violated the Fair Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”)—and cost him three jobs—when it prepared

three background reports about him that included inaccurate information about his criminal past.

He’s wrong. HireRight’s reports were accurate. In fact, the relevant state court docket shows to

this day that HireRight reported exactly what the court is displaying about Plaintiff’s criminal

record. For this reason, Plaintiff’s claims against HireRight fail as a matter of law, and HireRight

is entitled to judgment.

I.      Background

        A.     Plaintiff’s relevant criminal history

        In 2016, Plaintiff was charged with a South Carolina felony for manufacturing or

possessing a controlled substance with the intent to distribute. See South Carolina v. Keith Eugene

Waters, No. 2015A3310100506 (Marion County General Sessions Court) (case docket attached as

Id. He was sentenced to three years in prison, with that term suspended in favor of one year of

probation. Id. He began his probation on February 12, 2018. Id. Today, the public Marion County

docket for his case still reflects a disposition of “Pled Guilty.” Id.

       B.      Plaintiff’s allegations3

       Plaintiff alleges that on December 1, 2021, he obtained a pardon for the felony drug

conviction from the South Carolina Department of Probation, Parole, and Pardon Services. Compl.

¶¶ 7–8. Shortly afterward, he applied to work for three trucking companies: Big G Express, Inc.;

Magnum LTD; and Ceva Logistics US, Inc. Id. ¶ 2. Those companies requested consumer reports

(background check reports) about Plaintiff from HireRight, a consumer reporting agency (“CRA”).

Id. ¶ 3. HireRight prepared a report for each company. Exhibit B (Dec. 31, 2021 report to

Magnum LTD); Exhibit C (Dec. 31, 2021 report to Big G Express, Inc.); Exhibit D (Jan. 4, 2022

report to Ceva Logistics).4

       The three reports reflect Plaintiff’s Marion County felony drug record and the disposition

of “pled guilty”—exactly what the case docket shows. Exs. B, C, D. The docket includes nothing

II.      Legal standard

         “After the pleadings are closed—but early enough not to delay trial—a party may move

for judgment on the pleadings.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(c). A court applies the same standard to a Rule

12(c) motion and a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim. Belmora, 819 F.3d

at 705. Thus, to avoid judgment for the defendant, the plaintiff must allege enough facts in the

complaint to support a plausible entitlement to relief. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)

(citing Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). While a court takes his factual

allegations as true, bare allegations of wrongdoing, legal conclusions, and a “formulaic recitation

of the elements of a cause of action” cannot support claims for relief. Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555;

Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 679. When only well-pleaded allegations remain and “there is no substantive

dispute that warrants the litigants and the court proceeding further,” judgment on the pleadings is

III.   Argument

       The Court should enter judgment for HireRight on Plaintiff’s FCRA claims because they

require allegations of inaccurate or outdated information. But what HireRight reported was—and

is—an accurate reflection of the current Marion County case docket. Its three reports were neither

inaccurate nor outdated. Therefore, Plaintiff’s FCRA claims cannot survive.

       A.      Plaintiff’s § 1681e(b) claim fails because HireRight’s reports were accurate.

       The FCRA exists to prevent CRAs from reporting inaccurate information about consumers,

so CRAs must maintain “reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the

information” they report. 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b). In the Fourth Circuit, a report is “accurate” when

it is not “patently incorrect or . . . misleading in such a way and to such an extent that it can be

expected to have an adverse effect.” Dalton v. Capital Assoc. Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d 409, 415 (4th

Cir. 2001). To state a claim that a CRA violated the accuracy requirement of § 1681e(b), a plaintiff

must first show that its report was inaccurate. Id.

       Plaintiff cannot do that here. That’s because HireRight’s three reports were both factually

true and unlikely to cause any misunderstanding about Plaintiff’s criminal history. HireRight

reported that Plaintiff was charged in 2016 with a felony for manufacturing or possessing a

controlled substance with an intent to distribute. That is true. Compare Exs. B, C, and D (reports)

with Ex. A (docket). HireRight reported that Plaintiff pleaded guilty to that charge in 2018. True.

Id. HireRight further reported that Plaintiff was sentenced to probation for one year. Also true.

Id. The reports, which match the Marion County case docket for Plaintiff’s drug conviction,

contain no factual inaccuracy or confusing information; they are 100% accurate.

       Established FCRA case law confirms that HireRight’s reports are accurate as a matter of

law. For decades, courts have agreed that a CRA reporting what a public court docket reflects is

not liable under the FCRA—even if the court’s information is wrong. E.g., Henson v. CSC Credit

Servs., 29 F.3d 280, 285–86 (7th Cir. 1994) (“[A]s a matter of law, a [CRA] is not liable under the

FCRA for reporting inaccurate information obtained from a court’s Judgment Docket, absent prior

notice from the consumer that the information may be inaccurate.”); Hammoud v. Experian Info.

Sols., Inc., No. 19-13262, 2021 WL 5106366, at *12 (E.D. Mich. July 23, 2021) (holding report

was accurate although it included information from incorrect bankruptcy court orders), aff’d sub

nom. Hammoud v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC, 52 F.4th 669 (6th Cir. 2022); Houston v. TRW Info.

Servs., Inc., No. 88-CIV-0186 (MEL), 1989 WL 59850, at *1–2 (S.D.N.Y. May 2, 1989) (holding

report was accurate when it included a judgment from the public court docket, although the

judgment had been vacated and the docket did not include the vacatur), aff’d sub nom. Houston v.

TRW Info., 896 F.2d 543 (2d Cir. 1990); Rhodes v. First Advantage Background Servs. Corp., No.

6:22-CV-2399-RBD-RMN, 2024 WL 1526155, at *8–11 (M.D. Fla. Mar. 5, 2024) (holding report

was accurate when it included a felony conviction, although the plaintiff had completed his

probation, the record was deemed dismissed under state law, and the court docket should have

been updated). This Court should likewise hold that HireRight is not liable for reporting exactly

what it obtained from the Marion County case docket—and what that docket still reflects.

       Allowing Plaintiff’s inaccuracy claim to go forward requires accepting his premise that

CRAs should second-guess a reliable public record source—including the original source of

criminal information—on the off chance that the court has not updated its docket for some reason.

See Compl. ¶ 14 (claiming HireRight failed to “verify public records”). That premise is neither

textually supported nor sensible.

       CRAs preparing consumer reports for employment, housing, and other benefits must

maintain “reasonable procedures” to assure maximum possible accuracy. 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b).

The FCRA does not require perfection. Dalton, 257 F.3d at 217. In the background screening

context, where the FCRA helps to “facilitate commerce,” requiring CRAs to look behind reliable

sources would drastically increase the “economic costs of investigation [and] render it

economically infeasible for CRAs to function.        Nothing in the FCRA or its implementing

regulations evinces the intent to place such heavy burdens on CRAs.” Jianqing Wu v. Trans Union,

Civ. A. No. AW–03–1290, 2006 WL 4729755 at *7 (D. Md. May 2, 2006), aff’d, 219 F. App’x

320 (4th Cir. 2007). And, practically speaking, “reliance on official court records is unlikely to

lead to inaccurate . . . reporting.” Henson, 29 F.3d at 285. “Requiring credit reporting agencies to

look beyond the face of every court document to find the rare case when a document incorrectly

reports the result of the underlying action would be unduly burdensome and inefficient.” Id. at

285–86.

       So, as one court put it, CRAs comply with the FCRA “so long as they accurately report

what they have been told.” Smith v. Auto Mashers, Inc., 85 F. Supp. 2d 638, 641 (W.D. Va. 2000).

HireRight did that here. Its reports were—and are—accurate according to the Marion County case

docket, and Plaintiff’s § 1681e(b) claim fails as a matter of law.

       B.      Plaintiff’s § 1681k(a) claim fails because he has not pleaded that HireRight
               failed to comply with both subsections and because HireRight’s reports were
               not incomplete or outdated.

       For a similar reason, Plaintiff cannot prevail on his § 1681k(a) claim against HireRight for

failing to ensure its reports to the trucking companies were “complete and up to date.” Compl.

¶¶ 34–35.

       In § 1681k(a), the FCRA aims to prevent CRAs from reporting incomplete or outdated

public record information that could affect a consumer’s employment. A CRA satisfies this

   4:23-cv-06668-SAL          Date Filed 08/01/24        Entry Number 24-1          Page 7 of 9




directive by either timely “notify[ing] the consumer of the fact that public record information is

being reported,” § 1681k(a)(1), or maintaining “strict procedures designed to ensure that whenever

public record information which is likely to have an adverse effect on a consumer’s ability to

obtain employment is reported it is complete and up to date,” § 1681k(a)(2). Therefore, to state a

§ 1681k(a) claim, the plaintiff must allege both that 1) the CRA failed to satisfy both subsections

of § 1681k(a) and that 2) the CRA’s reporting was not “complete and up to date.” See Henderson

v. CoreLogic Nat’l Background Data, LLC, No. 3:12CV97, 2016 WL 4611570, at *5 (E.D. Va.

Sept. 2, 2016) (requiring allegations that a report was “incomplete or outdated to prevail on a claim

under [§ 1681k(a)]”); Henderson v. Source for Pub. Data, L.P., 53 F.4th 110, 125 (4th Cir. 2022)

(same). Plaintiff has satisfied neither pleading requirement here.

       First, Plaintiff simply asserts (without support) that HireRight failed to “use strict

procedures to ensure the reported information [was] complete and up to date” under § 1681k(a)(2).

Compl. ¶ 35. Nowhere does he also allege that HireRight failed to notify him of adverse

information appearing on his consumer reports under § 1681k(a)(1). Thus, he has not sufficiently

alleged that HireRight violated § 1681k(a). See Source for Pub. Data, 53 F.4th at 125 & n.19

(noting liability exists only if the CRA complied with neither subsection). For that reason alone,

Count 2 fails as a matter of law.

       Second, Plaintiff’s § 1681k(a) claim under either subsection fails because HireRight’s

reports were not incomplete or outdated. Obabueki v. Int’l Bus. Machs. Corp., 145 F. Supp. 2d

371, 396 (S.D.N.Y. 2001) (“The logical starting point for an analysis of Section 1681k is whether

the information provided was complete and up to date.”). The FCRA says that public records of

convictions—like Plaintiff’s felony drug conviction—are “up to date if the current public record

status of the item at the time of the report is reported.” 15 U.S.C. § 1681k(a)(2); see also Jones v.

Sterling Infosystems, Inc., 317 F.R.D. 404, 409 (S.D.N.Y. 2016) (“The plain language of section

1681k(a)(2) supports interpreting ‘complete’ in the context of the currency of the reported public

records.”).

         Here, HireRight reported the current public record status of Plaintiff’s felony drug

conviction: he pleaded guilty in February 2018. Exs. B, C, D. Per the docket, that information

remains current, complete, and up-to-date today. Ex. A. Nothing about HireRight’s reporting of

that record was incomplete or outdated. See Kelly v. Bus. Info. Grp., Inc., No. CV 15-6668, 2016

WL 7404470, at *7 (E.D. Pa. Dec. 22, 2016) (granting 12(c) motion on § 1681k claim because the

plaintiff did not sufficiently allege the CRA reported “something other than the current status of

that court case”). Therefore, HireRight is entitled to judgment on Count 2.

II.      Conclusion

         Because HireRight’s reports about Plaintiff were accurate, complete, and up-to-date

according to the Marion County court docket, Plaintiff cannot state claims under the FCRA. The

Court should grant HireRight’s motion for judgment on the pleadings and enter judgment in

HireRight’s favor on Plaintiff’s claims.
