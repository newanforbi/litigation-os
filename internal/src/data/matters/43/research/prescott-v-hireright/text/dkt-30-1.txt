I. INTRODUCTION

The Court should dismiss Plaintiff's two-count Complaint against HireRight
Solutions, Inc. (“HireRight Solutions”) for alleged “willful” violations of the Fair
Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”) (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) because Plaintiff has not
pled enough facts to support any FCRA violations, let alone willful ones.

Plaintiffs Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) centers on his failed criminal
background check for his former employer, Advanced Towing, in February 2011. At
that time, Plaintiff worked as a tow truck driver for Advanced Towing. Advanced
Towing had a contract with Defendant Automobile Club of Southern California
(“Auto Club”) to provide towing services to the Auto Club’s members. Advanced
Towing’s tow truck drivers were required to pass a criminal background check to
provide such services on behalf of Advanced Towing to the Auto Club’s members.
Plaintiff, ani ex-felon, was unable to do so, and Advanced Towing terminated his
employment.'

Plaintiff does not allege any employment relationship with HireRight Solutions.
Rather, Plaintiff alleges that HireRight Solutions is the consumer reporting agency
(“CRA”) that compiled the criminal background report for his employer, Advanced
Towing, and then “adjudicated” the report for Advanced Towing (i.e., assessed the
report based on the Auto Club’s eligibility criteria). Still, Plaintiff contends that
HireRight Solutions willfully violated one of the FCRA’s employment-related notice
provisions, Section 604(b)(3) (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)).”

Plaintiff's core premise — that in February 2011 HireRight Solutions had, and
willfully disregarded, an independent pre-adverse action notice obligation to him
under Section 604(b)(3) — is flat out wrong.

The plain text of the FCRA sensibly imposes on the employer, not the CRA, the
duty to provide a pre-adverse action notice to the consumer (i.e., the job applicant).
The consumer is expecting to hear back from the employer about its ultimate
employment decision (to hire or retain, or not) and may be unaware even of the
CRA’s existence or identity in the employment screening process. For that reason
alone, the purpose animating Section 604(b)(3) — affording the consumer with an
opportunity'to discuss the report with the employer before the employer takes adverse
action — is in no way furthered by requiring pre-adverse action notices from both the
employer and the CRA. To the contrary, a judicially-imposed requirement to provide
such redundant notification could thoroughly confuse the consumer.*

Furthermore, under the U.S. Supreme Court’s landmark opinion in Safeco Ins.
Co. of Am. v. Burr, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), a willful FCRA violation claim fails on the
face of the Complaint where, as here, the defendant’s reading of the FCRA is
supported by the statutory text. Indeed, at all times material to the SAC, ail of the
available authorities supported (and, to this day, continue to support) HireRight
Solutions’ reading of Section 604(b)(3). In addition, because under Safeco HireRight
Solutions’ alleged “subjective bad faith” is entirely immaterial, the Court should

dismiss the SAC with prejudice. In Safeco, the Supreme Court expressly cautioned

Ui. STATUTORY OVERVIEW

The FCRA regulates the exchange of consumer information between a CRA
and a user. that procures a consumer report for “employment purposes” (i.e., an
employer or prospective employer).® With respect to each —- CRA and employer — the
FCRA imposes distinct and independent obligations.

Examples of the FCRA obligations that are specific to CRAs in the employment
context include conditions that relate to CRAs providing such reports to employers
(15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1)) and that impose limitations on CRAs for compiling such
reports for employers (15 U.S.C. §§ 1681a(g)(1), 1681d(d)(2) and 1681k).

Examples of the obligations that are specific to employers include conditions on
employers that relate to the procurement of such reports from CRAs (15 U.S.C. §
1681b(b)(2)) and directives to employers to make certain statutory disclosures to}
current and prospective employees (15 U.S.C. §§ 1681a(y)(2) and 1681b(b)).

Section 604 (15 U.S.C. § 1681b) — the FCRA provision invoked by Plaintiff in
his SAC — sets out in separate paragraphs the distinct and independent obligations of
CRAs and employers, respectively.

Pursuant to Section 604(a), a CRA may only furnish a consumer report to a user
with a “permissible purpose” (¢.g., to an employer that “intends to use the information
for employrhent purposes”). Pursuant to Section 604(b)(1), a CRA may only furnish a
consumer report to a user for employment purposes if the CRA first receives
certification from the employer that it will comply with its obligations under Section

604(b)(2) (e.g., provide timely pre-adverse action notice).

IV. LEGAL STANDARD

The standard for reviewing a motion for judgment on the pleadings is the same
as the standard for a motion to dismiss. Dworkin v. Hustler Magazine Inc., 867 F.2d
1188, 1192’(9th Cir. 1989). Rule 12(b)(6) authorizes dismissal if the Complaint fails
to plead “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Fed. R.
Civ. P. 12(b)(6); Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007); accord
Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). “[A] plaintiff's obligation to provide the
‘grounds’ of his ‘entitle[ment] to relief? requires more than labels and conclusions,
and a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.” Twombly,
550 U.S. at 555. The Court need not accept Plaintiffs legal conclusions as true:
“‘Tt}hreadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere
conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id.
