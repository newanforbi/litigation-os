 Factual Background

      The SAC alleges the following facts: The Auto Club contracts with tow truck
 companies to provide roadside assistance services to its members. (SAC ¶ 13). In or
 about 2011, the Auto Club initiated a policy of requiring all technicians who provide
  services to its members to pass a criminal background check based on the Auto Club’s
  criteria. (SAC ¶ 28). This new policy applied not only to new technicians, but to all
  existing technicians. (SAC ¶ 29). On information and belief, the SAC alleges that the
 Auto Club’s screening criteria disqualified anyone with a felony conviction. (SAC ¶
  65).

      Under this new policy, the Auto Club paid HireRight to be the exclusive
  provider of consumer reports. (SAC ¶ 30). HireRight not only provided consumer
  reports regarding technicians, it also adjudicated each technician’s eligibility by
 comparing his or her criminal history information to the criteria provided by the Auto
 Club. (SAC ¶ 31). In making this assessment, a technician could be adjudicated as
 “does not meet company standards” or “meets company standards.” (SAC ¶ 32). The
 SAC alleges that the technician is not provided an opportunity to dispute the
  adjudication because it is sent privately to the towing station. (SAC ¶ 35).

          If the technician is adjudicated as “does not meet company standards,” the Auto
 Club automatically sends a notification to the towing company that the technician is
 denied access to the Auto Club’s members. (SAC ¶¶ 33, 36). The Auto Club further
  requires towing companies to terminate technicians who do not pass the background
 check. (SAC ¶ 34). The SAC asserts that failure to terminate such technicians would
  “likely result” in the termination of the towing station’s contract with the Auto Club.
 (SAC ¶ 34). Prescott’s main contention is that neither HireRight nor the Auto Club
 provided notice to technicians before adjudicating them or before denying them access
  to Auto Club members. (SAC ¶ 46). Instead, the two companies informed the towing

  First Request for Judicial Notice

      The Auto Club also filed a Request for Judicial Notice in Support of Motion to
 Dismiss Second Amended Complaint Pursuant to FRCP 12(b)(6) (the “First Request”).
 (Docket No. 31-1). The Auto Club asks that the Court take judicial notice of the pre-
 adverse action letter that AT sent to Prescott on February14, 2011 (“AT’s Letter”).
 (Request at 1).

      “As a general rule, [a court] ‘may not consider any material beyond the
 pleadings in ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.’” United States v. Corinthian Colls.,
 655 F.3d 984, 998 (9th Cir. 2011) (quoting Lee v. City of Los Angeles, 250 F.3d 668,
 688 (9th Cir. 2001)). However, “a district court ruling on a motion to dismiss may
 consider a document the authenticity of which is not contested, and upon which the
  plaintiff’s complaint necessarily relies.” Parrino v. FHP, Inc., 146 F.3d 699, 706 (9th
  Cir. 1998) (superseded by statute on unrelated grounds). The policy underlying this
  rule is to “[p]reven[t] plaintiffs from surviving a Rule 12(b)(6) motion by deliberately
 omitting references to documents upon which their claims are based.” Id.

      AT’s Letter meets the requirements for judicial notice. As an initial matter,
  Prescott does not dispute its authenticity.

       Moreover, the letter is relevant, in so far as the SAC relies on the non-existence
  of a pre-adverse action notice that complied with the FCRA’s requirements. Other
  courts have considered extrinsic documents as necessary to a plaintiff’s claims, where
  the documents potentially bar the plaintiff’s claims and the plaintiff does not dispute
  the authenticity of the documents. See, e.g., Birdsong v. AT & T Corp., No. C12-6175
 TEH, 2013 WL 1120783, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 18, 2013) (considering a release
 agreement not referenced in the complaint because the release is integral to the
  plaintiff’s allegations in that it may bar her claims, and because the plaintiff does not
  dispute that she signed it and argued extensively in the opposition that the release
 agreement was unenforceable); Bhandari v. Capital One, N.A., No. 12-04533 PSG,
 2013 WL 1736789, at *3-4 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 22, 2013) (considering whether a waiver
  provision in a Forbearance Agreement barred the plaintiffs’ instant claims, on a motion
  to dismiss, where the plaintiffs “acknowledge signing the Forbearance Agreement and
  the concomitant release,” and did not allege in their First Amended Complaint that the
  provision was unenforceable). Accordingly, it is in this sense that the SAC relies on
 AT’s pre-adverse action notice.

       Furthermore, “the primary problem raised by looking to documents outside the
 complaint—lack of notice to the plaintiff—is dissipated [w]here plaintiff has actual
  notice” of those documents. Hotel Employees & Rest. Employees Local 2 v. Vista Inn
 Mgmt. Co., 393 F. Supp. 2d 972, 979 (N.D. Cal. 2005) (finding that the Court could
 not consider a purchase agreement on a motion to dismiss because plaintiff was not a
  party to the agreement and claimed to have no notice of it). Prescott clearly had notice
  of AT’s Letter because he was the one who produced it to Defendants. (Request at 1).

        Therefore, the First Request is GRANTED.

  the Court, and asserts that this third agreement disclaims any obligation on the Auto

      As noted above, “a district court ruling on a motion to dismiss may consider a
 document the authenticity of which is not contested, and upon which the plaintiff’s
 complaint necessarily relies,” without converting the motion into one for summary
 judgment. Parrino, 146 F.3d at 706 (9th Cir. 1998).  However, Prescott has asserted
  that the SAC does not necessarily rely on the two service agreements submitted by the
 Auto Club, but may be based on a third service agreement or some other discoverable
 agreement. On this basis alone, the Second Request must be denied.

      The Request for Leave further demonstrates that the Auto Club’s evidentiary
  requests are not limited to evidence upon which the SAC necessarily relies.  Instead,
  the Auto Club seeks to demonstrate that Prescott cannot possibly establish the
  allegations in the SAC. While the Auto Club may be correct, this factual inquiry is
 more appropriate for a motion for summary judgment. See United States v. Ritchie,
 342 F.3d 903, 907 (9th Cir. 2003) (“When ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss,
  if a district court considers evidence outside the pleadings, it must normally convert the
  12(b)(6) motion to a Rule 56 motion for summary judgment . . . .”); Lee, 250 F.3d at
 680 (finding that the district court erred in taking judicial notice of disputed facts).
 This Court is simply unwilling to convert the Auto Club’s Motion into one for
 summary judgment, without first permitting Prescott the opportunity to conduct
  discovery.

        Therefore, the Second Request and the Request for Leave are both DENIED.

      The Auto Club has brought a motion to dismiss the SAC under Federal Rule of
  Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). In ruling on the Motion under Federal Rule of Civil
 Procedure 12(b)(6), the Court follows Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544,
 127 S. Ct. 1955, 167 L. Ed. 2d 929 (2007), and Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 129 S.
  Ct. 1937, 173 L. Ed. 2d 868 (2009). “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must
  contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is
  plausible on its face.’” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citation omitted). “All allegations of
  material fact in the complaint are taken as true and construed in the light most
  favorable to the plaintiff.” Williams v. Gerber Prods. Co., 552 F.3d 934, 937 (9th Cir.
 2008) (holding that a plaintiff had plausibly stated that a label referring to a product
  containing no fruit juice as “fruit juice snacks” may be misleading to a reasonable
 consumer).

       HireRight has brought a motion for judgment on the pleadings with regard to the
 SAC. “After the pleadings are closed—but early enough not to delay trial—a party
 may move for judgment on the pleadings.” F. R. Civ. P. 12(c). “Judgment on the
 pleadings is properly granted when[, accepting all factual allegations in the complaint
  as true,] there is no issue of material fact in dispute, and the moving party is entitled to
 judgment as a matter of law.” Fleming v. Pickard, 581 F.3d 922, 925 (9th Cir. 2009).
 “Although Iqbal establishes the standard for deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, we have
  said that Rule 12(c) is ‘functionally identical’ to Rule 12(b)(6) and that ‘the same
 standard of review’ applies to motions brought under either rule.” Cafasso, U.S. ex rel.
  v. Gen. Dynamics C4 Sys., Inc., 637 F.3d 1047, 1054 n.4 (9th Cir. 2011).

       Because both motions are reviewed under the same standard and both motions
  raise overlapping arguments, they will be analyzed together.

           The Lamson Holding

        In the February 10 Order, the Court dismissed the FAC’s pre-adverse action
  notice claim in large part based on Lamson v. EMS Energy Marketing Service, Inc.,
 868 F. Supp. 2d 804, 810 (E.D. Wis. 2012). The February 10 Order read Lamson as
 holding that only an employer was required to provide pre-adverse action notice under
 § 1681b(b)(3)(A), and that the common law test of agency applies to determine
 whether an employment relationship exists for the purpose of the FCRA. (See Feb. 10
 Order at 5-7).

        Prescott argues for the first time that Lamson does not hold that only employers
  are required to provide pre-adverse action notice. (Opp. to Auto Club at 4). Instead,
  Prescott argues that Lamson held that if a report is not used for employment purposes,

       The Court disagrees. The court in Lamson simply did not need to consult the
  definition of a consumer report in its analysis. The provision requiring pre-adverse
  action notice limits itself to a “consumer report for employment purposes.” 15 U.S.C.
  § 1681b(b)(3)(A) (emphasis added). The FCRA provides that “[t]he term
  ‘employment purposes’ when used in connection with a consumer report means a
  report used for the purpose of evaluating a consumer for employment, promotion,
  reassignment or retention as an employee.” 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(h) (emphasis added).
  The district court thus concluded that to establish a FCRA violation, the defendant
  must have obtained the consumer report to evaluate the plaintiff “for a position as an
  employee of” the defendant company. Lamson, 868 F. Supp. 2d at 810. Therefore, the
  Court reached this conclusion without referring to the definition of a consumer report
  and without asserting that its reasoning depended on the FCRA’s definition of the
  consumer report. Accordingly, the Lamson decision can be fairly read as requiring an
  employer-employee relationship into the pre-adverse action notice requirement itself.

            Scope of the FCRA’s Pre-Adverse Action Notice Requirement

       The more important question is whether the court in Lamson was correct in
  finding that the FCRA imposed the pre-adverse action notice requirement on
  employers only. The law appears to be unsettled on this issue.

      On the one hand, Prescott has cited to two cases holding that the pre-adverse
  action notice requirement is not limited to employers, but extends to a consumer
  reporting agency and a staffing agency that took adverse employment actions. See
  Goode, 848 F. Supp. 2d 532; Adams v. National Engineering Service Corp., 620 F.
  Supp. 2d 319 (D. Conn. 2009).

         Prescott additionally argues that these two cases are consistent with a Ninth
  Circuit case, Reynolds v. Hartford Financial Services Group, Inc., 435 F.3d 1081,
  1095 (9th Cir. 2006) rev’d on other grounds Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr,

      On the other hand, both Defendants argue that the FCRA imposes the pre-
  adverse action notice requirement on employers only. As discussed above, Defendants
  cite to Lamson, which interpreted § 1681b(b)(3)(A) as requiring an employer-
  employee relationship between the person intending to take an adverse action and the
  consumer. See Lamson, 868 F. Supp. 2d at 810, 816.

        Other the Lamson, however, Defendants have not provided persuasive authority
  demonstrating that § 1681b(b)(3)(A) is limited to employers.

        HireRight argues that two other sections of the FCRA indicate that Congress
  intended to impose the pre-adverse action notice requirement on the employer.

        HireRight points to the FCRA’s definition of “adverse action,” which includes
  “a denial of employment or any other decision for employment purposes that adversely
  affects any current or prospective employee,” 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(k)(1)(B)(ii).
  (HireRight’s Mot. at 8). HireRight argues that the words “current or prospective
  employee” demonstrates that the person intending to take the adverse action must be
  the employer. However, the Court does not find that reading persuasive. The text of
  the statute itself does not limit an adverse action to one taken by an employer. And as
  demonstrated in Adams, the person taking the adverse agency could be a staffing
  agency, rather than the direct employer, if the latter outsources its work to the former.

        HireRight next points to another section of the FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(y)(2),
  which requires an “employer” to disclose a summary of a communication upon which
  an adverse action is based, if that communication meets the criteria of 15 U.S.C. §
  1681a(y)(1). (HireRight’s Mot. at 9). However, § 1681a(y) appears to deal with
  communications that do not meet the definition of a consumer report. Therefore, the
  Court is not persuaded that this section sheds light on duties related to the use of
  consumer reports.

       Based on the legal authority provided by the parties, the Court finds that the
  plain text of § 1681b(b)(3)(A) does not limit the pre-adverse action notice requirement
  to employers. The statute imposes a duty on a “person intending to take” any adverse
  action based “in whole or in part” on a consumer report “for employment purposes.”
  15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A). The FCRA defines “employment purposes” to refer to
  situations when a consumer report is “used for the purpose of evaluating a consumer
  for employment, promotion, reassignment or retention as an employee.” 15 U.S.C. §
  1681a(h).

       While the Lamson court read the phrase “as an employee” to require an
  employer-employee relationship between the person intending to take the adverse
  action and the consumer, that reading is not necessitated by the plain text of the statute.
  The definition of “employment purposes” does not state that the consumer report must
  be used by an employer; instead, it uses the passive voice, thereby, omitting the subject
  who is using the consumer report.

         Similarly, the section requiring pre-adverse action imposes its duty on “a person
  intending to take” an adverse employment action. 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A). If
  Congress had so intended, it could have specifically used the word “employer,” but it
  did not. At least two other courts have thus read § 1681b(b)(3)(A) as imposing the pre-
  adverse action notice on entities other than employers. See Goode, 848 F. Supp. 2d
  532; Adams, 620 F. Supp. 2d 319. The Court is persuaded by the reasoning in Goode
  that the use of the word “person,” instead of employer in § 1681b(b)(3)(A) , indicates
  that the provision can apply to other entities.

        Therefore, the court does not read 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A) as imposing the
  pre-adverse action notice requirement on employers only.

           The Auto Club’s Adverse Employment Action

   still be liable under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A), if the SAC plausibly alleges that it
  took an adverse action. The duty in § 1681b(b)(3)(A) is triggered when a person
  “us[es] a consumer report for employment purposes,” and intends to take an adverse
  action against the consumer “based in whole or in part on the report.” See id.

       The Auto Club argues that its actions cannot constitute adverse actions within
  the meaning of the FCRA because the SAC does not allege that the Auto Club received
  or evaluated the technician’s consumer reports. (Auto Club’s Reply at 8).

  HireRight and “receive[s] confidential criminal history information from HireRight

  Auto Club automatically sends a notification to the towing company denying the

  used or expected to be used . . . for the purpose of serving as a factor in establishing the

  1081 (4th Cir. 1975). An adjudication stating that a technician does not meet certain

  “character, general reputation, or personal characteristics.” Id.

           The Pre-Adverse Action Notice Claim Against HireRight

        In the briefs and at the hearing, HireRight argued that the structure of the FCRA
  demonstrates Congress’s intent to impose distinct rather than shared obligations on
  consumer reporting agencies and users of consumer reports. (HireRight’s Mot. at 11-
  13; HireRight’s Reply at 4). This argument is strongly supported by the structure of
  the FCRA and case law. See, e.g., Obabueki, 145 F. Supp. 2d at 393-94 (finding that
  the first subsection of 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1) imposes obligations on consumer
  reporting agencies, while the second and third subsections impose obligations on
  users). This interpretation of the FCRA also potentially conflicts with the holding in
  Goode, 848 F. Supp. 2d at 542-43, that a consumer reporting agency may be required
  to provide pre-adverse action notice. However, the Court need not address this thorny
  issue. Nor does the Court need to determine whether HireRight took an adverse
  employment action against Prescott. Instead, the Court can dispose of the claim
  against HireRight based on the issue of willfulness.

       The SAC fails to plausibly allege that HireRight willfully violated §
  1681b(b)(3)(A). As Prescott concedes, it appears that no court prior to Goode found a
  consumer reporting agency liable for failing to provide pre-adverse action notice.
  (Opp. to HireRight at 4). Even the court in Goode declined to find that the consumer
  reporting agency there could have acted willfully because “what constitutes an adverse
  action under [the FCRA] is not always clear.” 848 F. Supp. 2d at 543. Therefore, it
  was not reckless for HireRight to believe that it had no duty to provide pre-adverse
  action notices under the FCRA. (Opp. to HireRight at 4).

       However, Prescott limits his concession that the SAC does not plausibly allege
  willfulness to the time period prior to Goode. (Opp. to HireRight at 4). The Court
  disagrees and finds that even actions post-Goode could not be considered reckless. As
  discussed above, while Goode held that a consumer reporting agency could be held
  liable under § 1681b(b)(3)(A), Lamson, which was decided shortly after Goode, held

        Class Allegations

       The Auto Club argues that the SAC’s class allegations are insufficient as a
  matter of law because they are not pled with particularity, as required by Twombly.
  (Auto Club’s Mot. at 23-25; Auto Club’s Reply at 21). The Court disagrees.

       The SAC alleges with particularity the Auto Club’s and HireRight’s general
  practices with regard to the adjudication of technicians, and the failure to provide pre-
  adverse action notice. While the Court may strike class allegations were it is “plain
  enough from the pleadings” to determine that a class cannot be certified, Sanders v.
  Apple Inc., 672 F. Supp. 2d 978, 990 (N.D. Cal. 2009), this is not such a case.
