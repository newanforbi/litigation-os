The End of AI-Hallucinated Citations

Free Westlaw Access at Your Fingertips

Brendan Ngwa Nforbi
Oct 08, 2026

AI can write a brief in minutes. It can also fill that brief with cases that don’t exist.

That isn’t a hypothetical. In Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), lawyers were sanctioned for filing a brief built on citations an AI tool had invented. The cases looked real. The quotes read well. None of it was there. Those were licensed attorneys. A self-represented litigant using the same tools runs the same risk, and the signature on the filing is yours.

The fix is not to stop using AI. The fix is to check every citation in a real research database, with a citator that tells you whether the case exists, says what you think it says, and is still good law. Lawyers do that with Westlaw or Lexis. Most self-represented people do it with Google, if they do it at all.

What most of us don’t know is that a number of public law libraries pay for a version of Westlaw that you can use from home, for free. It is called Westlaw Patron Access Remote. The library puts a link on its website, you register, and if a seat is open, you’re in.

I couldn’t find a master list of these libraries, so I built one and checked every entry against the library’s own website. The directory is at the end of this piece. First, what this program is, what it isn’t, and how to use it without wasting a scarce seat.

Three different things called “free Westlaw”

People use “free Westlaw” to mean three different things. They are not interchangeable.

Westlaw Patron Access is the version most law libraries have. It runs on terminals inside the library. You walk in, sit down, and search. You can’t take it home. Thurston County’s law library in Washington puts it plainly: “Westlaw may only be accessed onsite.” The Massachusetts Trial Court Law Libraries say the same about all 15 of their locations.

Westlaw Patron Access Remote is a separate subscription that some libraries buy on top of that. Thomson Reuters’ own product terms say it is available only to government law libraries and libraries open to the public. Users reach it by clicking a link on the library’s website and accepting a license agreement. The library buys a fixed number of “seats,” usually one to three. A seat is one person signed in at a time, not one account. When the seats are full, you get a message telling you to try again later.

The 7-day trial is a sales trial. Thomson Reuters offers a free seven-day trial of Westlaw Edge on its website, and says it doesn’t ask for a credit card. A handful of libraries point patrons to it, sometimes with instructions for registering as a library user. It’s useful for one intense week, but it is not continuing access.

Most of the libraries in my directory describe their remote access as part of a “pilot program” with Thomson Reuters. Pilots end. Subscriptions lapse when budgets get cut. That is why every entry below links to the library’s own page and not to a Westlaw login screen.

What checking a citation actually takes

In my last piece, You Don’t Need a Lawyer, I argued that AI has moved the bottleneck in self-representation from producing legal text to verifying it. Anyone can generate a confident brief now. The advantage goes to whoever can check it. Every case you cite has to exist, say what you claim, come from the court you name, and still be good law.

You can do some of that with free tools. Court websites publish opinions. Legislatures publish statutes. But free tools are thin on the two things that make verification fast.

The first is a citator. Westlaw’s citator, KeyCite, is the core of the product. It tells you whether a case has been reversed, overruled, or questioned, and it shows you what has cited it since. The Massachusetts Trial Court Law Libraries describe KeyCite as a way “to determine if a citation is still good law,” and the Wisconsin State Law Library lists it as part of its subscription. Those are both in-library services. Remote plans are separate subscriptions and vary from library to library, so check your library’s plan before you count on any feature. When a citator is available, it is how you find out in two minutes whether a case an AI tool handed you can carry any weight.

The second is secondary sources: treatises, practice guides, and legal encyclopedias that explain a whole area of law and point you to the controlling authority. In California, that means books like Witkin’s Summary of California Law and California Jurisprudence. These cost real money, and they are often the fastest route to the elements of a claim.

Here I need to be careful. Remote plans are usually smaller than what the library has on-site. San Diego Law Library says its remote program “contains all primary State and Federal law, but our on-site Westlaw contains more secondary sources.” Hillsborough County in Florida says the same thing in almost the same words. San Diego’s in-library Westlaw includes Witkin, Cal Jur, and Miller & Starr; its remote plan is mainly primary law. Riverside County’s posted title list for its remote service, on the other hand, does include Witkin, Cal Jur, California Civil Practice, and Miller & Starr.

So don’t assume. If you need a specific treatise or feature, check the library’s content list or ask a librarian before you plan your research around it. If the remote plan doesn’t have it, the on-site terminals often do.

How to use it well

The details vary by library, so read your library’s page first. These tips come straight from those pages.

Always enter through the library’s link. San Diego warns that “if you go directly to Westlaw to log in, it will not let you log in.” The system checks that you arrived from the library’s website. The Law Library of Louisiana adds that private or incognito browser windows won’t work for the same reason. Bookmark the library’s page, not the Westlaw page it sends you to.

Register for OnePass first, and use a fresh email. OnePass is Thomson Reuters’ sign-in system. Expect to verify your email and set up two-factor authentication with your phone. Several libraries, including San Diego, New Hampshire, and Hays County, warn that an email address you have used with Westlaw before may be rejected or blocked. Use one that Thomson Reuters has never seen.

Expect a busy signal. With one to three seats serving a whole county, sometimes a whole state, you will sometimes be told to try later. Try off-hours. If you are on a deadline, don’t wait until the last night to start.

Sign off. Every time. This is the most important habit on the list. If you just close the browser, your seat stays occupied for 7 to 10 minutes at most libraries, and 10 to 15 at San Diego and New Hampshire. Click Sign Off and the next person gets in right away.

Watch your download count. San Diego and New Hampshire both cap downloads and emails at 20 documents per session and 150 per day, shared across both seats. Once you hit the cap, you can still print, but you can’t download. Hillsborough also caps downloads without publishing the numbers. Save what you actually need. Don’t bulk-download a hundred cases “just in case.”

Plan short sessions. Imperial County, which has a single seat, limits each user to 30 to 45 minutes. Even where there is no stated limit, go in with a list. Know which citations you’re checking and which questions you’re answering before you sign in.

Respect the eligibility rules. Most programs are for self-represented people and non-attorneys. Some allow attorneys only when they are not working for a client. Louisiana, Kalamazoo, and Pima exclude practicing attorneys outright. Some require residency: Pima County residents, Cook County residents, New Hampshire residents. New Hampshire also requires a short request form, and the library replies within one business day. Several libraries say plainly that any other use is not allowed. Lane County describes Oregon’s program as for non-commercial use.

Use the free help. Several library pages list Thomson Reuters’ reference attorney line, 1-800-733-2889, for help building searches, and its technical support line, 1-800-934-9378, for login problems. Library staff can show you how the tools work, but they can’t give legal advice.

Etiquette, and why it matters

These seats are scarce, and they are not free to the library. Public money pays for them: county budgets, court fees, sometimes state or federal library grants. The point is to let people without lawyers find the law. Treat yours that way.

Keep your sessions short and focused. Sign off. Don’t share your login. Thomson Reuters’ published product terms allow it to suspend individual users for “excessive use.” If you don’t live in a library’s service area and it has a residency rule, don’t use that library. Find your own.

And thank your library. I mean that practically. These programs survive on usage numbers and on someone telling the board they matter. Riverside County’s annual reports track remote Westlaw sessions: 3,606 in 2023–24. In Florida, Volusia County’s remote program was cancelled this fall after a county budget vote cut the law library’s funding. If remote Westlaw helped your case, send the library a short note saying so. A librarian can take that to a budget hearing.

How this directory was checked

There is no master list of libraries with remote Westlaw, at least none I could find from Thomson Reuters or anyone else. So I checked every entry against the library’s own website, or an official court or county page, on Oct 8, 2026. If I couldn’t confirm a program on an official page, I left it out.

That mattered. Volusia County’s law library homepage now says that after a September 22, 2026 county budget vote, “Remote access to Westlaw via vclawlib.org is canceled.” The old remote-access page is still online. If that was the only page you found, you would think the program was live.

Details drift, too. Clark County, Nevada’s web page says its subscription allows three remote users. An older guide from the same library says two. I went with the current web page, but that kind of disagreement is common.

It is the same discipline as checking a citation. Old pages and secondhand lists go stale. Go to the source. Programs change, so confirm with the library before you rely on one.

The directory

Each entry links to the library’s own page. Seats are concurrent users. “Not stated” means the library’s page doesn’t give a number, not that access is unlimited.

Remote access (Westlaw Patron Access Remote)

Arizona

  • Law Library and Resource Center, Superior Court in Pima County (Tucson). For Pima County residents who are self-represented or non-attorneys. The page says attorneys are not permitted. Seats: 2. Westlaw Pro Se

Arkansas

  • Pulaski County Law Library, housed at the UA Little Rock Bowen School of Law library (Little Rock). For pro se patrons and self-represented litigants. Seats: not stated. Access only through the link near the bottom of the page. Law Library research page

California

  • Imperial County Law Library (El Centro). For self-represented patrons; the page describes patrons in Imperial County logging in from home. Seats: 1. Time limit 30 to 45 minutes per user. Westlaw Remote Patron Access

  • Riverside County Law Library (Riverside, Palm Springs, Temecula). For pro se patrons and non-attorneys. Seats: 2. The posted remote title list includes Witkin, Cal Jur, California Civil Practice, and Miller & Starr. Legal Databases & eBooks

  • San Diego Law Library. For self-represented litigants and non-attorneys. Seats: 2. Downloads capped at 20 per session and 150 per day across both seats. The remote plan is mainly primary law; more secondary sources on-site. Westlaw Remote for Pro Se

Florida

  • Hillsborough County Public Library Cooperative (James J. Lunsford Law Library, Tampa). For self-represented litigants, non-attorneys, and attorneys not working for a client. Seats: 2. Session and daily download caps. The remote plan is mainly primary law. Westlaw Patron Access Remote

Hawaii

  • Hawaiʻi State Law Library System. “Intended for expanded public or pro se access to the law.” Seats: not stated. Scroll to the Westlaw entry. Databases

Illinois

  • Cook County Law Library (Chicago). For Cook County residents. Seats: 2, per the county’s Westlaw contract. The link is at the bottom of the page. Legal Research Resources

Louisiana

  • Law Library of Louisiana (New Orleans). For self-represented people who are not attorneys. Seats: 1. Private browser windows won’t work. Library Westlaw

Michigan

  • Kalamazoo Public Library. For pro se patrons only; attorneys use Westlaw at the Central Library. Seats: not stated. Westlaw

Minnesota

  • Anoka County Law Library. For pro se patrons. Seats: not stated. Remote Access to Westlaw

  • Hennepin County Law Library (Minneapolis). For self-represented litigants, non-attorneys, and attorneys not working for a client. Seats: 2. Get Remote Access to Westlaw

  • Otter Tail County Law Library (Fergus Falls). For pro se patrons. Seats: not stated. Law Library

  • Wright County Law Library (Buffalo). Links to Westlaw Patron Access Remote “through the Ramsey County Law Library” for self-represented people. Seats: not stated. Ramsey County’s own site doesn’t mention the program, so call if you have trouble. Law Library

Nevada

  • Clark County Law Library (Las Vegas). For self-represented litigants, non-attorneys, and attorneys not working for a client. Seats: 3. The library building has been closed to the public since July 2, 2026, with temporary in-person help at the West and East Las Vegas libraries. Westlaw Remote Access

New Hampshire

  • New Hampshire Law Library (Concord). For New Hampshire residents who are not attorneys; retired or former attorneys may use it for personal matters. Seats: 2. Submit a request form first; the library replies within one business day. Download caps of 20 per session and 150 per day. Westlaw Patron Remote

Oregon

  • State of Oregon Law Library (Salem). For pro se litigants and non-attorneys; county law libraries describe it as available to all Oregonians for non-commercial use. Seats: not stated. Includes state and federal law, law reviews, and treatises; some items are “Out of Plan.” Westlaw (Remote)

Texas

  • Harris County Robert W. Hainsworth Law Library (Houston). For self-represented people and non-attorneys. Seats: 2. The library also runs LAWPods, private booths with Westlaw and other databases at several Harris County Public Library branches. Remote Database Access

  • Hays County Law Library (San Marcos). Designed for pro se patrons. Seats: 1. Register with an email that isn’t tied to any other Westlaw account. Westlaw Patron Access Remote Access

7-day trial routes

These are one-time trials, not continuing access. Several libraries note that the email you register with must be new to Thomson Reuters.

  • Thomson Reuters offers a free 7-day Westlaw Edge trial directly and says it requires no credit card. Trial page

  • Loudoun County Public Library (Virginia). Links to the 7-day trial and tells you to select “Library User” when registering. Online resources

  • Central Rappahannock Regional Library (Fredericksburg, Virginia). A one-time 7-day remote pass; unlimited Westlaw inside its branches. Westlaw

  • Fulton County Courts Justice Resource Center (Atlanta). Free 7-day Westlaw Edge trial for patrons, plus monthly Westlaw classes taught by a Thomson Reuters lawyer. Legal Research

  • Dell DeHay Law Library of Tarrant County (Fort Worth, Texas). Points patrons to the Thomson Reuters trial and has public Westlaw on-site. Law Library Services

In-person terminals

Many more law libraries offer free Westlaw on public computers inside the library. This is the ordinary Patron Access product, and it is often the fuller version. A few verified examples:

  • Massachusetts Trial Court Law Libraries: Westlaw, Lexis, and VitalLaw free at all 15 locations.

  • Cook County Law Library, Chicago: Westlaw, Lexis, and HeinOnline at the main library and branches.

  • New Castle County and Sussex County Law Libraries, Delaware: Westlaw, Lexis Advance, and HeinOnline.

  • Wisconsin State Law Library, Madison: Westlaw with KeyCite, including on your own device over the library’s Wi-Fi.

  • LA Law Library partner locations, including Compton Library and Pasadena Public Library.

  • Alabama Supreme Court and State Law Library, Kentucky State Law Library (by appointment), and the State Library of Iowa.

  • In Virginia, the Walter T. McCarthy Law Library in Arlington and the Alexandria Law Library.

  • Smaller setups count too: a Westlaw laptop at Keene Public Library in New Hampshire, terminals at Siouxland’s Downtown Library in Sioux Falls, and Westlaw at eight Vermont public libraries through Vermont Law School’s Community Legal Information Center.

If your library isn’t listed, call your county law library and ask two questions: do you have public Westlaw, and do you offer remote access for self-represented people? You may find a program I missed.

Corrections and additions

If you know of a library I missed, or something here has changed, tell me in the comments or send me a message with a link to the library’s page. I’ll verify it and update the list.
