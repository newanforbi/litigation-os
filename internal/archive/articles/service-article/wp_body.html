Most self-represented cases don't die in a courtroom. They stall at the front door, with the wrong person, the wrong form, or the wrong date.

In You Don't Need a Lawyer: The Fundamentals of Self-Representation in 21st Century Civil Litigation, I argued that ordinary people can run their own civil cases. I still believe that. But I skipped the place where most pro se cases quietly stall. It isn't the hearing. It's service of process.

"Service of process" means formally delivering the lawsuit to the person or company you're suing, in a way the law accepts, and then proving it to the court. Until you do, the defendant has no deadline to respond and you can't get a default. Your case just sits there.

I've served a truck dealer, a national retailer, several carriers, and individuals, in California state court and federal court. Some sheriff returns came back clean in four days. Other times a clerk declined my default request, a sheriff bounced my form the day it arrived, or a server handed papers to the wrong person at the right address. Here is what those files taught me, in the order you'll go through it.

1. Who to serve

Start with bizfile, not the address you know

If you're suing a business, search it on the California Secretary of State's site, bizfileonline.sos.ca.gov. Write down three things: the exact legal name, the entity type, and the agent for service of process. That's the person or company officially designated to receive lawsuits for the business. Note the agent's address and county too.

Under Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) § 416.10, you serve a corporation by delivering the summons and complaint to that agent. You can also deliver them to someone the statute lists: the president or other head, a vice president, secretary, treasurer, controller or CFO, a general manager, or a person the corporation has authorized to receive service.

In my truck-dealer case, bizfile showed two different individual agents at two different addresses, one for each related company. An earlier attempt had gone to the seller's old lot for the newer company. That wasn't service on its agent. It was paper left at a building.

The name on the sign is not the entity

The sign over the door is often just a trade name. In that same dispute there were two corporations, formed years apart, with similar names, each with its own agent. Handing papers to a manager at the lot they shared did not serve the corporation whose agent lived in another county.

Capacity matters as much as address

"Capacity" means the role in which someone is served: as themselves, or on behalf of a company. Under CCP § 412.30, the summons you serve on a corporation or unincorporated association has to tell the person served that they're receiving it on behalf of that entity, and under which statute. On the Judicial Council summons form, that's the "Notice to the Person Served" box. If the notice is missing, no default can be taken against the entity. Fill it in every time, for example: the entity's name plus "CCP 416.10 (corporation)."

Corporate agents vs. individual agents

Many large companies use a professional registered agent such as CSC or CT Corporation System. These companies file a certificate with the Secretary of State naming the employees authorized to accept service at each office (Corporations Code § 1505). That usually means a front desk and a predictable return. Smaller businesses often name an individual, like an owner or manager. That's valid, but your server has to find that one person.

2. Who can serve

In California, any person at least 18 years old who is not a party to the case may serve a summons (CCP § 414.10). Federal court has the same rule (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure (FRCP) 4(c)(2)). That means you never serve your own papers, not even by signing as the sender of a mailing. The Judicial Council's notice-and-acknowledgment form (POS-015) says the sender "must not be a party in this case."

You have three options: the sheriff or marshal, a registered process server, or any adult non-party.

The sheriff, especially with a fee waiver

If the court granted you a fee waiver (form FW-003 or FW-005), sheriff and marshal fees are waived too (California Rules of Court, rule 3.55). Fill out form SER-001, Request for Sheriff to Serve Court Papers. Give it to the sheriff in the county where the defendant or its agent is located, along with copies of everything to be served and a copy of your fee waiver. Don't file SER-001 with the court. It's confidential and goes only to the sheriff.

In two carrier cases, I had a fee waiver and sent SER-001 to the sheriff where each registered agent sat. One agent was CSC and the other was CT Corporation. Both were served within four days. Each return named the authorized employee and cited CCP § 416.10, and I paid no fee because of the waiver.

Later, a private server quoted me a flat fee to serve two entities at the same CT Corporation address. If you don't have a fee waiver, sheriff fees vary, so ask the sheriff's civil office or your court's self-help center.

Fill in SER-001 the way the sheriff reads it

One sheriff's office rejected my SER-001 the day it arrived. I had filled in both item 3b(2) (a specific person to serve) and item 3b(3) (the agent for service). That office wanted 3b(2) left blank whenever 3b(3) names an agent. The form itself doesn't say so, and other offices may differ. Still, when you serve through a registered agent, the safe move is to fill in 3b(3) and leave 3b(2) blank. (Item numbers are from the SER-001 revised January 1, 2026.)

In federal court, if you're allowed to proceed in forma pauperis (the federal fee waiver), the court must order service by the U.S. Marshal or a specially appointed person (FRCP 4(c)(3)).

3. Methods

Personal service

The server hands the summons and complaint to the person to be served (CCP § 415.10). Service is complete on delivery. For a company, that person is the agent or someone listed in § 416.10.

Substitute service: two steps

When personal delivery doesn't work, CCP § 415.20 allows substitute service. It always takes two steps: leave the papers with a qualifying adult, then mail a copy (first-class, postage prepaid) to the person at the place where the papers were left.

  • § 415.20(a) covers entities and the people listed in § 416.10. Leave the papers during usual office hours with the person apparently in charge, then mail.
  • § 415.20(b) covers individuals, and only after personal delivery fails despite "reasonable diligence." Leave the papers at the person's home, usual workplace, or usual mailing address (not a P.O. box). They have to go to a competent household member or a person apparently in charge, at least 18, who is told what the papers are. Then mail.

Either way, service is complete on the 10th day after mailing, not the day the papers were left.

In one case, a registered process server made two attempts and then left the papers with an HR person at the defendant's workplace. The invoice was $134. The mailing under § 415.20(b) went out later, with a certificate of mailing. I filed a default request dated from the day the papers were left, and it was declined. The defendant's 30 days hadn't even started yet.

Coming January 1, 2027: a new version of § 415.20 takes effect. For individuals, it defines reasonable diligence as good-faith attempts at personal delivery on at least three occasions, on three different days, at three different times. It also allows the mailing to go Priority Mail with tracking or Certified Mail with return receipt, in addition to first-class mail.

Notice and Acknowledgment (CCP § 415.30)

You can serve by mail in California, but only if the other side signs. A non-party sender mails the summons and complaint, two copies of the Notice and Acknowledgment of Receipt (POS-015), and a postage-paid return envelope. Service is complete on the date the defendant signs the acknowledgment, as long as it comes back. If it doesn't come back within 20 days, you serve another way, and the court can make the defendant pay for that.

I offered one in a meet-and-confer draft. It's cheap and shows good faith, but it isn't service until the signed form is in your hands.

Federal waiver (FRCP 4(d))

The federal version is a request to waive service. Send the complaint, two copies of the official waiver form, and a prepaid way to return it, and give the defendant at least 30 days. A defendant who signs gets 60 days from the date you sent the request to respond. One who refuses without good cause can be charged your later service costs. Once you file the signed waiver, you don't need a separate proof of service.

Acceptance only counts when it's on paper

In one case, a defendant accepted service through counsel on a stated date. In another, a signed stipulation vacated a default and waived the defendant's challenges to service. Both worked because they were written, signed, and specific. "We'll accept" on a phone call doesn't count.

4. Proof of service

Service you can't prove doesn't help you. In California civil cases, the proof is usually form POS-010, and each party served needs its own. Before you file it, check that it shows:

  • Who was served. The party's name as it appears on the documents (item 3a), plus the actual person who took the papers if that's someone else (item 3b).
  • In what capacity. As an individual, or on behalf of an entity under a specific section such as 416.10 (item 6).
  • The method (item 5). For substitute service, include the name and title of the person the papers were left with, which box applies, and the mailing date. For an individual, attach a declaration of diligence.
  • Every document served (item 2): summons, complaint, ADR package, and anything else. In general civil cases, the plaintiff must serve the court's ADR information package with the complaint (California Rules of Court, rule 3.221(c)).
  • The date, the address, and the server's signature.

Why clerks decline defaults

A clerk can't fill in gaps for you. A clerk declined two of my default requests. In the first, my papers didn't show the documents were left with a qualifying person. In the second, the proof didn't list the documents served. A postage receipt or a "served" line on the docket doesn't replace that list.

The right lot but the wrong person

One server went to the correct business lot and served a manager, and the proof checked the box for service on an individual. That return doesn't start the corporation's clock, and I wouldn't request a default on it. The next attempt has to name the agent, and the proof has to match.

5. Deadlines

California: 60 days. California Rules of Court, rule 3.110(b) requires you to serve every named defendant and file the proofs within 60 days after filing the complaint. A defendant added by amendment gets 30 days. If you need more time, apply before the deadline, with a declaration describing your efforts (rule 3.110(e)). Behind that is a hard outer limit: three years to serve, or dismissal is mandatory (CCP §§ 583.210, 583.250).

Don't hold service for a caption fix. In one case, the e-filing system listed the Doe defendants first and produced the wrong short title. The issued summons still named the actual companies. Serve by the 60th day and file your proofs anyway.

Federal: 90 days. Under FRCP 4(m), if a defendant isn't served within 90 days after the complaint is filed, the court must dismiss that defendant without prejudice or order service by a set date. It must extend the time if you show good cause.

After removal, don't restart a clock that already ran. "Removal" is when a defendant moves your state case to federal court. Federal law lets you finish incomplete or defective service afterward (28 U.S.C. § 1448), but a defendant who was properly served stays served. In one carrier case, the sheriff served before removal. The federal scheduling order put the job of serving that order on the defendant, and Rule 4(m) wasn't running against me. In a separate federal case against an individual, FRCP 4(e)(1) let me use California's substitute-service method. California's mailing rule still controlled when service was complete.

Count the response time from completion. A California defendant has 30 days after service to respond (CCP § 412.20(a)(3)). Count from when service was complete. That's the delivery date for personal service, the 10th day after mailing for substitute service, and the signing date for an acknowledgment.

6. When it goes wrong

It will, at least once.

  • Read every return the day it arrives. If the person, capacity, method, documents, or date is wrong, don't build on it.
  • Re-serve rather than defend a shaky service. A clean second service is usually faster.
  • Go back to bizfile. Most of my problems were the wrong entity or the wrong agent, not the wrong street.
  • Don't treat an unstamped proof as filed. I had one with no clerk's stamp. Until it's stamped, assume the court doesn't have it.
  • Fix a rejected sheriff form and resubmit right away. Keep copies of both versions.
  • Ask for more time before the deadline, not after.

The checklist I use now

  1. Confirm the exact entity name on bizfile. Don't go by the sign or a trade name.
  2. Print the agent record: the agent's name, address, and county.
  3. Choose your server: the sheriff or marshal if you have a fee waiver, otherwise a registered process server or an adult non-party. The plaintiff never serves.
  4. Pick the method. Try personal service first. Use substitute service only after diligent attempts, plus the mailing. Use notice and acknowledgment (CCP § 415.30) or a Rule 4(d) waiver only if the other side signs.
  5. List every document served: summons, complaint, ADR package, and any case-management papers.
  6. Complete the "Notice to the Person Served" with the entity's name and the right section (for example, CCP § 416.10).
  7. On SER-001, leave the individual box (3b(2)) blank if you named an agent in 3b(3).
  8. Calendar the deadline: 60 days in California state court (CRC 3.110(b)), 90 days in federal court (FRCP 4(m)).
  9. Check the return for the person, capacity, documents, and date. For substitute service, add 10 days after mailing.
  10. File the proof of service and confirm it's stamped.
  11. Start the response clock from the date service was complete.
