## Glossary of terms used in this case

- **Pro se:** Latin for "for oneself"; describes a party who handles a case without a lawyer.
- **Docket:** the numbered, running log of every filing and order in a lawsuit.
- **ECF No.:** the entry number a document receives in Electronic Case Filing, the federal courts' intake system. ECF No. 29 here is the twenty-ninth entry.
- **Complaint:** the opening document of a lawsuit, naming the defendants and stating the plaintiff's reasons for suing.
- **In forma pauperis (IFP):** a request to go forward without paying the filing fee up front because money is limited.
- **Protective order:** an order limiting how certain information or people are handled during a case.
- **Summons:** a clerk-issued notice telling a defendant about the suit and the time allowed to respond.
- **Service of process:** formal delivery of the summons and complaint to a defendant.
- **Motion to dismiss:** a request that the court end some or all claims without a trial, frequently arguing that the complaint fails to state a legal claim even if every fact in it is accepted.
- **Opposition / reply:** the brief answering a motion, and the moving side's answer to that brief.
- **Joint case management statement:** a status report with proposed scheduling, filed before a conference with the judge.
- **Case management conference:** a session where the judge and parties discuss the schedule, discovery, and the path forward.
- **Magistrate judge:** a federal judicial officer chosen by a court's district judges for a fixed term.
- **Nature of suit:** a statistical category drawn from the civil cover sheet; it does not determine the actual claims.
- **42 U.S.C. § 1983:** an 1871 federal statute allowing suits against anyone who, using state authority, allegedly deprived a person of federal constitutional or statutory rights.

## Legal background, in general terms

The Fourth Amendment requires searches and seizures to be reasonable. Courts typically measure a short investigative stop against "reasonable suspicion" and an arrest against the stricter "probable cause." The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause guards against losing life, liberty, or property without fair procedure, and its Equal Protection Clause generally demands similar treatment of people in similar situations.

Two general rules shape who can be sued under § 1983. Under *Monell*, a local government is not liable just because it employs a wrongdoer; the harm must be tied to a policy, custom, or comparable decision. And the Supreme Court has held that states and state agencies are not "persons" subject to damages suits under § 1983. These principles explain why § 1983 litigation often turns on whom a plaintiff sues and in what capacity. The court's own reasoning in this case is in the F&R linked above.

On a motion under Rule 12(b)(6), the question is whether the complaint, read with its well-pleaded facts taken as true, states a plausible claim, the standard from *Twombly* (2007) and *Iqbal* (2009). Legal conclusions get no such presumption, and pleadings drafted by self-represented parties are construed liberally.

## The doctrines in general terms

None of what follows describes the court's analysis in this case; it is general background.

*Color of state law.* A § 1983 plaintiff generally must show a federal right was violated by someone using authority granted by the state. Whether a private organization qualifies as a state actor is decided case by case, and this guide takes no position on that issue here.

*Retaliation.* Courts in the Ninth Circuit usually ask three things: whether the plaintiff engaged in protected activity; whether the defendant's action would discourage a person of ordinary firmness from continuing it; and whether the protected activity was a substantial or motivating factor behind the action.

*Equal Protection.* A disparate-treatment claim generally requires facts showing different treatment from others similarly situated, combined with a discriminatory intent or purpose. Racial classifications by government draw strict scrutiny, the most demanding form of judicial review.

*Substantive due process.* Courts read this doctrine narrowly. When executive officials are involved, the Supreme Court has framed the test as whether their conduct is so arbitrary that it "shocks the conscience."

*Qualified immunity.* Individual officials facing § 1983 damages claims may invoke this defense, which applies unless they violated a clearly established right. The record reviewed does not say whether anyone raised it here.

*Pleading and amendment.* Dismissal for failure to state a claim falls under Rule 12(b)(6), and challenges to service under Rule 12(b)(5). *Twombly* (2007) and *Iqbal* (2009) require enough factual content to make each claim plausible; bare legal conclusions fall short. ## Background: the FCRA in employment screening

The following describes the statute in general terms, not the allegations in this case.

- *Up-front notice and consent.* Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2), an employer that wants a consumer report on an applicant or worker generally has to hand over a clear, written, stand-alone disclosure and get the person's written permission first.
- *Warning before acting on a report.* Section 1681b(b)(3) generally requires an employer, before taking adverse action that relies even partly on a report, to share the report and a summary of the person's rights under the statute.
- *Getting it right.* Reporting agencies have to follow reasonable procedures aimed at the highest feasible accuracy (§ 1681e(b)) and must look again at information a consumer disputes (§ 1681i).
- *Damages and deadlines.* Willful and negligent violations can lead to damages under §§ 1681n and 1681o, while § 1681p sets time limits for suing and allows the case to be filed in either state or federal court.
