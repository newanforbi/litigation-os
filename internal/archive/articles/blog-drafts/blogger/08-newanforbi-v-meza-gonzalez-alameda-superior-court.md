## Glossary of terms used in this case

- **Superior Court:** California's general trial court; every county has one.
- **In pro per (in propria persona):** representing oneself; federal courts usually say *pro se*.
- **Complaint:** the filing that starts a civil case by naming the parties, describing what the plaintiff says happened, and listing the legal claims and the relief requested.
- **Cause of action:** a single legal claim within a complaint.
- **Damages:** money awarded to make up for harm.
- **Tort:** a civil wrong (contract disputes aside) that can lead to an award of money.
- **False imprisonment:** under California law, unlawfully restraining someone's freedom of movement without consent.
- **Battery:** touching someone in a harmful or offensive way without permission; claims against police generally turn on whether the force crossed the line into unreasonable.
- **Bane Act:** the common name for Civil Code § 52.1, the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act, which permits suits against anyone who interferes or attempts to interfere with a person's federal or state rights by threats, intimidation, or coercion.
- **Demand for jury trial:** a formal request that a jury, rather than a judge, decide the facts.
- **Sued individually:** the claim targets the person, not only the agency.
- **Doe defendants:** placeholder names California procedure allows for people the plaintiff cannot yet identify, who may later be substituted by their real names.
- **Register of actions:** the state-court term for a docket.
- **Direct calendar:** a setup where a single judge oversees a case for most purposes.

## What the complaint alleges

The complaint calls itself "a state tort action." It describes an encounter with CHP officers in Alameda County on November 1, 2024 that, by the complaint's account, started as a welfare check. Newanforbi alleges he was detained, handcuffed, arrested, and transported to CHP's Dublin office, where blood was drawn, and afterward kept at Santa Rita Jail, with a parole hold extending how long he was held. The complaint also says no criminal charges were ever brought, and that CHP subsequently issued a detention certificate under Penal Code §§ 849/851.6. All of this is the plaintiff's account; no court has made findings on it.

A note on that certificate: Penal Code § 849 allows a person who was arrested to be released without appearing before a judge in certain circumstances, for example when officers conclude there is no basis for a criminal complaint. Section 851.6 provides that someone released in some of those circumstances receives a certificate describing the event as a detention, not an arrest.

The three causes of action are false imprisonment, battery, and violation of Civil Code § 52.1. The complaint also demands a jury trial. The named defendants are the State of California, Does 1–10, and the two officers, Officer Santiago Meza-Gonzalez and Officer Jose Melano, each named individually.

## The claims, in general legal terms

*False imprisonment.* California courts generally list the elements as confining someone intentionally, without consent or legal privilege, for an appreciable period, even a brief one. In cases about police detentions or arrests, whether the officer's conduct was privileged is often the key question.

*Battery by a peace officer.* An ordinary battery claim asks whether physical contact was harmful or offensive and lacked consent. Since the law sometimes authorizes officers to use reasonable force, a plaintiff bringing this claim against an officer generally has to prove the force went beyond what was reasonable.

*The Bane Act.* Civil Code § 52.1 is California's own civil rights statute. Unlike federal § 1983, it centers on interference "by threat, intimidation, or coercion." Appellate courts in the state have addressed how that element works in detention and arrest cases, including whether specific intent to violate rights must be shown. Remedies under the statute can include damages and, in some situations, civil penalties and attorney's fees.

*Fictitious names.* Code of Civil Procedure § 474 lets a plaintiff use a fictitious name for an unidentified defendant and swap in the true name by amendment later.

*Claims against public employees and entities.* California's Government Claims Act generally requires someone seeking money from a public entity, and often from employees for conduct within their job duties, to submit a written claim to the entity before suing, typically within six months for personal injury claims. This is described only in general terms; the Trellis page does not address it, and this guide does not address how it might apply here.

## Step-by-step: how the case has moved, and what usually comes next

**Step 1: Filing (February 4, 2026).** The complaint was filed in pro per. As of the Trellis page reviewed, this is the only step that appears.

**Step 2 (general): Service.** The plaintiff must arrange formal delivery of the summons and complaint to each defendant, known as service of process, and then file a proof of service showing it was done. Code of Civil Procedure § 583.210 sets an outer limit of three years after filing to serve, but the California Rules of Court generally call for proof of service within 60 days of filing.

**Step 3 (general): The response.** A defendant usually gets 30 days from service to file a response. That can be an answer, which admits or denies the allegations, or a demurrer, California's counterpart to a motion to dismiss, which tests the legal sufficiency of the complaint. Section 430.41 generally obliges a defendant planning a demurrer to talk with the plaintiff first.

**Step 4 (general): Case management.** The court usually sets a case management conference to discuss the schedule. Before it, each side typically submits Judicial Council form CM-110, the Case Management Statement, no later than 15 calendar days in advance.

## Finding the record

Unless a court seals them or a law makes them confidential, California trial court filings are generally open to the public. Trellis, a legal research platform, gathers and indexes state trial court documents, which is how this complaint is viewable online; UniCourt is another private service for California dockets.

The official source is the Alameda County Superior Court's eCourt Public Portal, which provides the register of actions, minutes, and hearing dates. Access needs a free account, and downloading document images costs a fee. Because no case number appears on the Trellis page, a search would normally begin with a party name, with the case number used for later lookups. Civil records can also be viewed in person on public computer terminals where the court keeps civil records. California dockets have no federal-style ECF numbers; entries appear by filing date and document title.

## Self-representation resources

Californians may represent themselves in civil cases, and people without lawyers are generally expected to meet the deadlines and procedures lawyers must follow, from the Code of Civil Procedure to the California Rules of Court and local rules. Every superior court has a self-help center, but coverage varies: Alameda's says general civil cases are outside what it helps with, although its site links to material on suing and being sued. The California Courts Self-Help Guide, published online by the Judicial Council, addresses many civil topics, and the Alameda County Law Library, lawyer referral services, legal aid organizations, and limited-scope attorneys are other resources.
