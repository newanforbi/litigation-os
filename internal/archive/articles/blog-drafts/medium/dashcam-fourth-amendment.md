Title: "I Think It's Booze": What a CHP Dashcam Recording Teaches About Your Fourth Amendment Rights
Subtitle: A sleeping driver, a flashlight, an opened door: a timestamped look at the legal questions one roadside encounter raises

At about 12:55 a.m. on November 1, 2024, a California Highway Patrol cruiser pulled onto the shoulder of Interstate 580 East in Livermore behind a parked rental Tesla. Inside was Brendan Ngehsi Newanforbi, a commercial truck driver who says he had pulled over to nap on his way home to Stockton. About sixteen minutes later he was in handcuffs. About forty minutes after that, he was driven away toward Santa Rita Jail.

Newanforbi later prepared a certified transcript of the CHP dashcam recording (CHP case RJ68845) and filed a civil complaint, *Newanforbi v. Meza-Gonzalez*, in Alameda County Superior Court on February 4, 2026, alleging false imprisonment, battery, and a violation of California's Tom Bane Civil Rights Act (Civil Code § 52.1). The case is pending. No court has made findings about what happened.

This article does not decide that case. It uses the transcript to show what the recording shows, what the law says, and which questions a court would have to answer. Quotes come from Newanforbi's transcript; bracketed descriptions are the transcript's own notations.

## Issue 1: Suspicion before contact

### What the recording shows

The first words on the audio come before anyone speaks to Newanforbi. At 00:55:53 Officer Melano says, "It's a rental. Huh." At 00:56:05 the transcript notes both officers "peering into Plaintiff's windows with flashlights, appearing to search the interior." At 00:56:09 Melano says, "I think it's booze." At 00:56:30 Melano adds, "Oh look at this." The transcript does not say what Melano was looking at.

### What the law says

Detaining someone requires more than curiosity. Under *Terry v. Ohio* (1968), a detention requires "specific and articulable facts" that, together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion. The Court contrasted this with an "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or 'hunch.'" A car being a rental, or an officer's guess before speaking to anyone, is not itself evidence of impairment.

### Why it matters

The recording raises the question whether later steps (opening the door, handling the cup, searching the back seat, the sobriety tests) rested on facts the officers gathered, or on a conclusion reached at 00:56:09, before anyone had spoken to the driver.

## Issue 2: The opened passenger door

### What the recording shows

Melano says "Open the window" at 00:56:40. Three seconds later the transcript records: [Passenger side door is opened by Officer Joselyn Melano.] The transcript records no request to open the door and no consent. The first exchange follows: "Hello." / "Uh-huh." / "Are you okay?" / "Yeah, we good."

### What the law says

Physically entering a car's interior is generally a search. In *New York v. Class* (1986), the Supreme Court held that an officer reaching into a car to move papers covering the VIN was a search, though a reasonable one, because it was narrow, the VIN carries no privacy interest, and the officer had seen traffic violations. The *plain view* doctrine (*Horton v. California*, 1990) lets officers seize what they can see from a lawful vantage point; it does not authorize opening a door to get a better view.

The government often relies on two justifications in situations like this, and both have limits:

- **Community caretaking.** *Cady v. Dombrowski* (1973) recognized that police perform duties "totally divorced" from crime-solving, including around vehicles. In *Caniglia v. Strom* (2021) the Court unanimously refused to extend that idea into the home and stressed that caretaking is not "an open-ended license" to act without a warrant. For cars, courts ask whether a genuine emergency made the specific intrusion reasonable, and whether the intrusion stayed within that concern.
- **Consent.** Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* (1973), consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. A sleeping person has consented to nothing.

### Why it matters

Knocking on a window is one thing; opening a door is a larger step. The transcript records no consent and no articulated emergency before the door was opened, and the first question asked, "Are you okay?", was answered "Yeah, we good." Whether that entry was justified is one of the central questions the recording raises.

## Issue 3: The cup and the back-seat search

### What the recording shows

After Newanforbi says he drank "Zero," Meza-Gonzalez asks about a cup. Newanforbi says, "Don't drink it. Yeah don't drink it." Melano says, "What is it? Let me see," then: "Is it water? No this is piss. This is pee." The transcript notes [Melano has liquid cup in hand and motions it.] Melano then says, "OK. Leave it there. Put it back."

At 00:59:46, as Newanforbi is walked to the patrol car, the transcript records Melano opening the rear passenger door and "conducting a search." This happens while Newanforbi is being escorted away from the car, and before any arrest.

### What the law says

*Arizona v. Hicks* (1987) held that even slightly moving an object to learn more about it, in that case turning stereo equipment to read serial numbers, is a separate search that requires probable cause. Picking up and handling a cup to learn what is in it is arguably similar.

Two exceptions usually justify warrantless car searches:

- **Automobile exception.** Since *Carroll v. United States* (1925), police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have *probable cause* to believe it contains evidence of a crime. A hunch, or even reasonable suspicion, is not enough.
- **Search incident to arrest.** *Arizona v. Gant* (2009) limits this to cases where the arrestee could reach the passenger compartment or it is reasonable to believe evidence of the offense of arrest is inside. Under the transcript's timestamps, Newanforbi was not handcuffed until 01:11:33, about eleven minutes after the back-seat search, and he was being walked away from the car when the rear door was opened.

### Why it matters

The cup held what the officer called urine, not alcohol. Whether there was probable cause at 00:59:46, when the back door was opened before any arrest and while Newanforbi was being escorted away, is a central question the recording raises.

## Issue 4: The exit order and the length of the detention

### What the recording shows

At 00:59:12: "Could I just have you uh just step out of your vehicle real quick?" Newanforbi complies. The transcript notes he was [calmly complying with commands, walking toward CHP vehicle in a manner consistent with no signs of impairment.] Questioning, testing, and arrest follow over the next twelve minutes.

### What the law says

In *Pennsylvania v. Mimms* (1977), the Court held that once a vehicle is lawfully stopped, officers may order the driver out as a safety measure. *Maryland v. Wilson* (1997) extended this to passengers. Both cases assume a lawful stop or detention to begin with. *Rodriguez v. United States* (2015) holds that a detention may last only as long as needed for its "mission," and that extending it for unrelated inquiries requires independent reasonable suspicion.

### Why it matters

If the purpose of the contact was to see whether the person inside was okay, the first answer was "Yeah, we good." The recording raises the question of what specific, articulable facts justified ordering him out and extending the encounter for another twelve minutes.

## Issue 5: Denials, coherence, and the escalation to sobriety tests

### What the recording shows

Newanforbi denies drinking at least six times ("Zero," "No, no," "No," "No sir," and others). His denials are consistent. He gives his name and spells it. He says he is in Livermore and reads a highway sign: "Isabella Avenue, Portola Avenue." He explains that, as a truck driver, he pulls over to nap when tired, and answers medical questions (pre-diabetes, no insulin, "No drugs or medicine"). He follows the officers' instructions. Meza-Gonzalez takes notes at 01:00:57 and 01:08:53, but the transcript does not record what he wrote.

### What the law says

Field sobriety tests are an investigative detention, so courts generally require reasonable suspicion of impairment. Odor of alcohol, slurred speech, and bloodshot eyes are common examples in DUI cases. The transcript records no officer statement about odor, speech, or eyes.

California law on DUI arrests of people found in parked cars also turns on specific facts and statutes, including what evidence shows that the car was driven. Those questions are fact-intensive and are for a court to resolve.

### Why it matters

A parked, sleeping driver who gives consistent denials, knows where he is, and reads road signs aloud presents a very different picture from a weaving driver. The recording raises the question of what signs of impairment, if any, the officers documented before escalating to sobriety tests.

## Issue 6: The tests, the breath-test advisement, and the arrest

### What the recording shows

Meza-Gonzalez gives an eye test and a 30-second time-estimation test. The transcript does not record how the officer scored either test. When the one-leg test comes up, Meza-Gonzalez tells Newanforbi plainly: "It's fine if you don't. You don't have to." Newanforbi asks whether he has passed. Melano: "Yeah there's not pass or fail." Melano also advises against the walking test: "it's on the side of the freeway and it's like uneven ground."

At 01:10:33 Meza-Gonzalez reads the preliminary alcohol screening (PAS) advisement: "You may refuse to take this test." Asked what happens if he refuses, the officer says: "it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not." At 01:11:15 Newanforbi calmly asserts the right he has just been told he has: "I don't consent to that." The next words are "turn around please. Place your hands behind your back," and he is handcuffed.

### What the law says

For most adult drivers in California, the roadside PAS is optional. Vehicle Code § 23612(i) requires officers to advise of "the person's right to refuse," and the officer here gave that advisement. Field sobriety tests are likewise voluntary, as the officer said. A warrantless arrest requires probable cause based on the totality of the circumstances.

### Why it matters

"You may refuse" and "I don't consent to that" are a textbook example of a person being told about a right and using it. The sequence raises the question Newanforbi's complaint presses: what facts, apart from his exercise of that right, supported probable cause at 01:11:16?

## Issue 7: After the arrest

### What the recording shows

Meza-Gonzalez searches Newanforbi's person ("I'm gonna just search you right now"). Newanforbi is placed in the patrol car with the handcuffs attached to the frame. Officers searched the car again after the arrest. When Newanforbi asks about Miranda, Melano explains: "We already have everything we need right? So we're not gonna read Miranda because we don't need anything." At 01:37:02 Melano adds: "Miranda rights we only give when we still wanna ask you questions about the investigation." The transcript mentions the vehicle being "stored" and ends at 01:57:10 on arrival at Santa Rita Jail.

### What the law says

- **Search of the person** incident to a *lawful* arrest is permitted (*United States v. Robinson*, 1973).
- **Inventory searches** of impounded cars must follow standardized procedures, and may not be a ruse for a general rummaging for evidence (*South Dakota v. Opperman*, 1976; *Florida v. Wells*, 1990).
- **Implied consent:** A driver *lawfully* arrested for DUI is deemed to have consented to a breath or blood test (Vehicle Code § 23612). Under *Birchfield v. North Dakota* (2016), breath tests may be required incident to arrest, but blood tests generally need a warrant. *Missouri v. McNeely* (2013) rejected an automatic exigency rule for blood draws.
- **Miranda** warnings are required for *custodial interrogation* (*Miranda v. Arizona*, 1966; *Berkemer v. McCarty*, 1984, on roadside questioning). Interrogation includes not only express questions but also words or actions officers should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response (*Rhode Island v. Innis*, 1980).

### Why it matters

Much of what follows 01:11:33 depends on whether the arrest was lawful. Search incident to arrest and implied consent both assume a lawful arrest, and inventory searches must follow standardized procedures rather than serve as a search for evidence. The officers' own statements about Miranda raise the question of how a handcuffed person in a patrol car should understand the questions put to him.

## Know your rights (general information)

- **You can say "I do not consent to searches."** Say it calmly, and don't physically resist. It preserves the issue for later.
- **You can remain silent** beyond identifying yourself where the law requires it. Say clearly, "I am choosing to remain silent."
- **Ask, "Am I being detained, or am I free to leave?"** The answer shapes your rights.
- **The roadside PAS is generally optional** for adult drivers who are not on DUI probation. **The post-arrest chemical test is not**; refusing it has DMV consequences.
- **Field sobriety tests are voluntary** in California, as the officer in this recording said.
- **Document everything** and talk to a lawyer about deadlines; claims against California agencies usually require a government claim first.

## FAQ

**Can police detain me because of a hunch?**
No. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, a detention requires specific, articulable facts, not an "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or 'hunch.'"

**Can an officer open my car door without a warrant?**
Physically entering a car is generally a search. A warrantless search has to fit a recognized exception, such as voluntary consent, probable cause under the automobile exception, or a genuine emergency, and the Supreme Court has made clear that community caretaking is not an open-ended warrant exception (*Caniglia v. Strom*).

**Do I have to take a roadside breath test in California?**
For most adults, no; officers must tell you that you may refuse. Saying "I don't consent" is exercising that right.

**Can I be arrested for DUI while parked?**
It depends. California law on DUI arrests of people found in parked cars turns on specific facts and statutes, including what evidence shows the car was driven. A lawyer can explain how those rules apply to a particular situation.

**When do officers have to read Miranda?**
Before custodial interrogation. Interrogation includes express questioning and also words or actions officers should know are reasonably likely to draw out an incriminating response (*Rhode Island v. Innis*).

## Key terms

- **Reasonable suspicion:** Specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity; required for a detention.
- **Probable cause:** A fair probability that a crime occurred or evidence will be found; required for most searches and arrests.
- **Automobile exception:** A warrantless vehicle search based on probable cause.
- **PAS:** Preliminary alcohol screening, a roadside breath test that is optional for most adults.
- **Implied consent:** A licensed driver's deemed agreement to chemical testing after a lawful DUI arrest.
