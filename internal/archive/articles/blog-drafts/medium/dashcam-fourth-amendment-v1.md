Title: "I Think It's Booze": What a CHP Dashcam Recording Teaches About Your Fourth Amendment Rights
Subtitle: A sleeping driver, a flashlight, an opened door: a timestamped look at the legal questions one roadside encounter raises

At about 12:55 a.m. on November 1, 2024, a California Highway Patrol cruiser pulled onto the shoulder of Interstate 580 East in Livermore behind a parked rental Tesla. Inside was Brendan Ngehsi Newanforbi, a commercial truck driver who says he had pulled over to nap on his way home to Stockton. About sixteen minutes later he was in handcuffs. About forty minutes after that, he was driven away toward Santa Rita Jail.

Newanforbi later prepared a certified transcript of the CHP dashcam recording (CHP case RJ68845) and filed a civil complaint, *Newanforbi v. Meza-Gonzalez*, in Alameda County Superior Court on February 4, 2026, alleging false imprisonment, battery, and a violation of California's Tom Bane Civil Rights Act (Civil Code § 52.1). The case is pending. No court has made findings about what happened, and the officers have not had their account tested in court either.

This article does not decide that case. It uses the transcript to show what the recording shows, what the law says, and which questions a court would have to answer. Quotes come from Newanforbi's transcript; bracketed descriptions are the transcript's own notations.

## Issue 1: Suspicion before contact

### What the recording shows

The first words on the audio come before anyone speaks to Newanforbi. At 00:55:53 Officer Melano says, "It's a rental. Huh." At 00:56:05 the transcript notes both officers "peering into Plaintiff's windows with flashlights, appearing to search the interior." At 00:56:09 Melano says, "I think it's booze." Melano adds, "It wasn't here earlier when we passed by," and at 00:56:30, "Oh look at this." The transcript does not say what Melano was looking at.

### What the law says

Officers may approach a parked car and knock without any suspicion. Shining a flashlight through a car window is not a "search": in *Texas v. Brown* (1983) the Supreme Court held that using a flashlight to look into a car's interior "trenched upon no right" under the Fourth Amendment. Officers are also allowed to check on a motorist who appears to be in trouble, and California law (Vehicle Code § 21718) generally bars stopping on a freeway except in emergencies and certain other situations. That gave officers a lawful reason to walk up.

Going further, by detaining someone, takes more. Under *Terry v. Ohio* (1968), a detention requires "specific and articulable facts" that, together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion. The Court contrasted this with an "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or 'hunch.'" A car being a rental, or an officer's guess before speaking to anyone, is not itself evidence of impairment.

### Why it matters

The question is whether later steps (opening the door, handling the cup, searching the back seat, the sobriety tests) rested on facts the officers gathered, or on a conclusion reached at 00:56:09.

## Issue 2: The opened passenger door

### What the recording shows

Melano says "Open the window" at 00:56:40. Three seconds later the transcript records: [Passenger side door is opened by Officer Joselyn Melano.] The transcript records no request to open the door and no consent. The first exchange follows: "Hello." / "Uh-huh." / "Are you okay?" / "Yeah, we good."

### What the law says

Physically entering a car's interior is generally a search. In *New York v. Class* (1986), the Supreme Court held that an officer reaching into a car to move papers covering the VIN was a search, but a reasonable one, because it was narrow, the VIN carries no privacy interest, and the officer had seen traffic violations. The *plain view* doctrine (*Horton v. California*, 1990) lets officers seize what they can see from a lawful vantage point; it does not authorize opening a door to get a better view.

The government often relies on two justifications in situations like this:

- **Community caretaking.** *Cady v. Dombrowski* (1973) recognized that police perform duties "totally divorced" from crime-solving, including around vehicles. In *Caniglia v. Strom* (2021) the Court unanimously refused to extend that idea into the home and stressed that caretaking is not "an open-ended license" to act without a warrant. For cars, courts ask whether an apparent emergency or welfare concern made the specific intrusion reasonable.
- **Consent.** Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* (1973), consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. A sleeping person has consented to nothing.

### Why it matters

Knocking on a sleeping driver's window is one thing; opening the door is a larger step. It could be read as a welfare check or as an entry with no consent and no articulated emergency. Newanforbi's complaint itself describes the encounter as beginning as a welfare check. Its theory is that the concern was resolved and the encounter escalated anyway.

## Issue 3: The cup and the back-seat search

### What the recording shows

After Newanforbi says he drank "Zero," Meza-Gonzalez asks about a cup. Newanforbi says, "Don't drink it. Yeah don't drink it." Melano says, "What is it? Let me see," then: "Is it water? No this is piss. This is pee." The transcript notes [Melano has liquid cup in hand and motions it.] Melano then says, "OK. Leave it there. Put it back."

At 00:59:46, as Newanforbi is walked to the patrol car, the transcript records Melano opening the rear passenger door and "conducting a search." Melano comes back with an empty can: "Mike's Hard Lemonade. Source alcohol." Newanforbi says he did not drink it.

### What the law says

*Arizona v. Hicks* (1987) held that even slightly moving an object to learn more about it, in that case turning stereo equipment to read serial numbers, is a separate search that requires probable cause. Picking up and smelling a cup is arguably similar.

Two exceptions usually justify warrantless car searches:

- **Automobile exception.** Since *Carroll v. United States* (1925), police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have *probable cause* to believe it contains evidence of a crime. A hunch or reasonable suspicion is not enough.
- **Search incident to arrest.** *Arizona v. Gant* (2009) limits this to cases where the arrestee could reach the passenger compartment or it is reasonable to believe evidence of the offense of arrest is inside. Under the transcript's timestamps, Newanforbi was not handcuffed until 01:11:33, about eleven minutes after the back-seat search.

California also has parole searches, which can permit searches of a parolee without suspicion (*Samson v. California*, 2006). But the California Supreme Court held in *People v. Sanders* (2003) that a search cannot be justified after the fact by a parole condition the officers did not know about. Dispatch did not report Newanforbi's parole status until 01:11:42.

### Why it matters

The cup held what the officer called urine, not alcohol; the empty can became the main physical evidence of alcohol. Whether there was probable cause when the back door was opened, before any arrest and before parole status was known, is a central question the recording raises.

## Issue 4: The exit order and the length of the detention

### What the recording shows

At 00:59:12: "Could I just have you uh just step out of your vehicle real quick?" Newanforbi complies. The transcript notes he was [calmly complying with commands, walking toward CHP vehicle in a manner consistent with no signs of impairment.] Questioning, testing, and arrest follow over the next twelve minutes.

### What the law says

In *Pennsylvania v. Mimms* (1977), the Court held that once a vehicle is lawfully stopped, officers may order the driver out as a safety measure. *Maryland v. Wilson* (1997) extended this to passengers. Both cases assume a lawful stop or detention to begin with. *Rodriguez v. United States* (2015) holds that a detention may last only as long as needed for its "mission," and that extending it for unrelated inquiries requires independent reasonable suspicion.

### Why it matters

If the encounter began as a welfare check, its mission was to confirm the person was okay, and "Are you okay?" was answered "Yeah, we good." Whether the cup, the can, the hour, and the party comment supplied new articulable facts is a judgment for a court.

## Issue 5: Denials, coherence, and the escalation to sobriety tests

### What the recording shows

Newanforbi denies drinking at least six times ("Zero," "No, no," "No," "No sir," and others). He gives his name and spells it. He says he is in Livermore and reads a highway sign: "Isabella Avenue, Portola Avenue." He explains that, as a truck driver, he pulls over to nap when tired, and answers medical questions (pre-diabetes, no insulin, "No drugs or medicine"). Meza-Gonzalez takes notes at 01:00:57 and 01:08:53, but the transcript does not record what he wrote.

Not every answer is crisp. At 00:57:49 the transcript notes [Plaintiff still groggy from sleep] when he answers "Two... two o'clock," and asked whether he had bumped his head, he says, "I'm not sure." He also told officers he came "from a party."

### What the law says

Field sobriety tests are an investigative detention, so courts generally require reasonable suspicion of impairment. Odor of alcohol, slurred speech, and bloodshot eyes are common examples in DUI cases. The transcript records no officer statement about odor, speech, or eyes. The medical questions are standard DUI-evaluation questions meant to rule out other explanations, and are not improper in themselves.

There is also the question of "driving." In *Mercer v. Department of Motor Vehicles* (1991), the California Supreme Court held that "drive" in the DUI statute requires proof of volitional movement of the vehicle. It also held that, under the arrest rules then in effect, an officer who did not see a parked car move could not make a lawful warrantless DUI arrest. The Legislature responded with Vehicle Code § 40300.5, which allows warrantless DUI arrests without observed driving in listed situations. Those include a person "observed in or about a vehicle that is obstructing a roadway." Driving can also be proven circumstantially, which may be why Meza-Gonzalez asked, "you were driving?" Newanforbi answered, "I was in the driver's seat, yes sir."

### Why it matters

A parked, sleeping driver is a harder DUI case than a weaving one. The answer turns on the "driving" question and on what signs of impairment the officers documented.

## Issue 6: The tests, the declined breath test, and the arrest

### What the recording shows

Meza-Gonzalez gives an eye test and a 30-second time-estimation test. The transcript's timestamps run from 01:08:02 to 01:08:40, about 38 seconds. The transcript does not record how the officer scored either test. When asked to stand on one leg, Newanforbi asks, "Really?" Meza-Gonzalez answers: "It's fine if you don't. You don't have to." Newanforbi asks whether he has passed. Melano: "Yeah there's not pass or fail." Melano also advises against the walking test: "it's on the side of the freeway and it's like uneven ground."

At 01:10:33 Meza-Gonzalez reads the preliminary alcohol screening (PAS) advisement: "You may refuse to take this test." Asked what happens if he refuses, the officer says: "it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not." At 01:11:15 Newanforbi says, "I don't consent to that." The next words are "turn around please. Place your hands behind your back," and he is handcuffed.

### What the law says

For most adult drivers in California, the roadside PAS is optional. Vehicle Code § 23612(i) requires officers to advise of "the person's right to refuse," and the officer here gave that advisement. A warrantless arrest requires probable cause based on the totality of the circumstances.

### Why it matters

The timing raises the question Newanforbi's complaint presses: what facts, apart from the declined optional test, supported probable cause at 01:11:16? The officers' report and testimony would supply the other side.

## Issue 7: After the arrest

### What the recording shows

Meza-Gonzalez searches Newanforbi's person ("I'm gonna just search you right now"). He asks, "What's this ankle bracelet for?" Newanforbi is placed in the patrol car with the handcuffs attached to the frame. The transcript records no strikes, takedowns, or other force beyond handcuffing. Melano asks what valuables should go on an inventory form and later searches the car, saying, "I need to make sure there's no guns. He's on parole." Newanforbi declines a chemical test after the implied-consent admonition. Melano says, "we're gonna get it anyways. It says it's going to be with a warrant." When Newanforbi asks whether this is a Miranda warning, Melano explains: "We already have everything we need right? So we're not gonna read Miranda because we don't need anything." The transcript mentions the vehicle being "stored" and ends at 01:57:10 on arrival at Santa Rita Jail.

### What the law says

- **Search of the person** incident to a lawful arrest is permitted (*United States v. Robinson*, 1973).
- **Inventory searches** of impounded cars must follow standardized procedures (*South Dakota v. Opperman*, 1976; *Florida v. Wells*, 1990). Known parole status can independently authorize a search (*Samson*).
- **Implied consent:** A driver *lawfully* arrested for DUI is deemed to have consented to a breath or blood test (Vehicle Code § 23612), and refusal carries DMV suspension. Under *Birchfield v. North Dakota* (2016), breath tests may be required incident to arrest, but blood tests generally need a warrant. *Missouri v. McNeely* (2013) rejected an automatic exigency rule for blood draws.
- **Miranda** warnings are required only for *custodial interrogation* (*Miranda v. Arizona*, 1966; *Berkemer v. McCarty*, 1984, on roadside questioning). Interrogation includes questions officers should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response (*Rhode Island v. Innis*, 1980). Reading the implied-consent advisement is generally not interrogation. A question to a handcuffed arrestee about an ankle monitor is a closer call.

### Why it matters

Much of what follows 01:11:33 depends on whether the arrest was lawful. Newanforbi's complaint alleges a warrant-based blood draw, that no criminal charges were filed, and that CHP later issued a Penal Code §§ 849/851.6 certificate describing the event as "a detention only." Those events are not in the dashcam transcript. They are allegations from the complaint.

## Know your rights (general information)

- **You can say "I do not consent to searches."** Say it calmly, and don't physically resist. It preserves the issue for later.
- **You can remain silent** beyond identifying yourself where the law requires it. Say clearly, "I am choosing to remain silent."
- **Ask, "Am I being detained, or am I free to leave?"** The answer shapes your rights.
- **The roadside PAS is generally optional** for adult drivers who are not on DUI probation. **The post-arrest chemical test is not**; refusing it has DMV consequences.
- **Field sobriety tests are voluntary** in California, as the officer in this recording said.
- **Document everything** and talk to a lawyer about deadlines; claims against California agencies usually require a government claim first.

## FAQ

**Is shining a flashlight into my car a search?**
No. Under *Texas v. Brown*, looking through a window, even with a flashlight, is not a search.

**Can an officer open my car door to check on me?**
Sometimes, if a genuine emergency or welfare concern justifies a limited intrusion that stays within that concern.

**Do I have to take a roadside breath test in California?**
For most adults, no; officers must tell you that you may refuse. After a lawful DUI arrest, refusing the chemical test leads to license suspension.

**Can I be arrested for DUI while parked?**
California requires proof of volitional movement (*Mercer*), but it can be shown by circumstantial evidence, and Vehicle Code § 40300.5 allows some arrests without observed driving.

**Didn't the officers have to read Miranda?**
Only before custodial interrogation. Many DUI arrests involve no post-arrest questioning, so no warnings are given.

## Key terms

- **Reasonable suspicion:** Specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity; required for a detention.
- **Probable cause:** A fair probability that a crime occurred or evidence will be found; required for most searches and arrests.
- **Automobile exception:** A warrantless vehicle search based on probable cause.
- **PAS:** Preliminary alcohol screening, a roadside breath test that is optional for most adults.
- **Implied consent:** A licensed driver's deemed agreement to chemical testing after a lawful DUI arrest.
