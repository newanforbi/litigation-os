## About the Eastern District of California

The Eastern District of California is one of California's four federal districts and covers 34 counties, stretching from the Oregon border through the Sacramento and San Joaquin Valleys to Kern County. It has two main divisions. The Sacramento division hears cases arising in counties including Sacramento, San Joaquin, Solano, Yolo, Placer, El Dorado, Shasta, and Butte, among others; the Fresno division hears cases from counties including Fresno, Kern, Tulare, Stanislaus, Merced, and Madera. There are also courthouses in Redding, Bakersfield, and Yosemite.

The Newanforbi case number begins with "2:", which marks a Sacramento case ("1:" marks Fresno). The "25" is the filing year, "cv" means civil, and the letters at the end, "DC" and "CSK," are the initials of the district judge and magistrate judge.

The Eastern District relies heavily on magistrate judges for pretrial work. Under its Local Rule 302(c)(21), cases in which a party is self-represented are referred to a magistrate judge for pretrial matters, which is why the "(PS)" label and two judges appear on the Newanforbi docket.

A **district judge** is nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and serves with life tenure. ## What the case is about

The court's April 20, 2026 Findings and Recommendations summarize the complaint this way. Newanforbi alleged that on November 1, 2024, at about 1 a.m., he was parked on a dirt shoulder off I-580 in Livermore when two CHP officers approached, investigated a suspected DUI, searched the car, and detained him. The complaint asserted Fourth Amendment, Fourteenth Amendment, and *Monell* claims.

In plain terms, the Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees due process and equal protection against state action. A ***Monell* claim**, named after a 1978 Supreme Court case, seeks to hold a government body itself responsible under § 1983 based on its policies or customs, rather than only the individual employees. These are allegations, not findings.

### Background: the constitutional claims in general

In general, the Fourth Amendment requires that searches and seizures be reasonable. Courts often analyze a brief investigative stop under the "reasonable suspicion" standard and an arrest under the higher "probable cause" standard. The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects against deprivations of life, liberty, or property without fair process, and its Equal Protection Clause generally requires the government to treat similarly situated people alike.

Under *Monell*, a local government cannot be held liable under § 1983 simply because it employs someone who committed a violation; the plaintiff must tie the harm to a policy, custom, or similar decision. Separately, the Supreme Court has held that states and state agencies are not "persons" who can be sued for damages under § 1983. These general rules help explain why parties in § 1983 cases often focus on who is sued and in what capacity. An **official-capacity** claim is treated as a claim against the government office itself, while an individual-capacity claim targets the person.

### The fee, IFP, and service steps

The $405 fee is the standard charge to open a civil case in federal district court. Because Newanforbi paid it, the case did not proceed IFP, and a fee-paid plaintiff is generally responsible for arranging service under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4. Rule 4(m) generally allows 90 days after the complaint is filed to serve each defendant.

### The pleading standard behind a motion to dismiss

A motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim, under Rule 12(b)(6), tests the complaint itself. Under the Supreme Court's decisions in *Twombly* (2007) and *Iqbal* (2009), a complaint must contain enough factual allegations to make a claim plausible. The court treats well-pleaded facts as true but not legal conclusions, and it reads a self-represented party's filings liberally.

## How findings and recommendations work

**Findings and recommendations** are a magistrate judge's proposed resolution of a matter the magistrate judge cannot finally decide alone. They are not a final ruling. Any party may file written **objections** within the deadline, and the other side may respond. The district judge then reviews de novo (fresh, without deference) any part that was objected to, and may accept, reject, or modify the recommendation.

This process comes from 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1) and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 72(b), which set a 14-day window for objections after service, and the Eastern District's Local Rule 304. In the Ninth Circuit, failing to file objections can limit what issues a party may later raise on appeal, which is why F&R documents usually include a warning to that effect.

## Representing yourself in federal court

Federal law (28 U.S.C. § 1654) allows parties to represent themselves in federal court. Self-represented litigants must follow the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the court's Local Rules, and in the Eastern District they file on paper unless the assigned judge permits electronic filing, which is the permission Newanforbi requested in November 2025.

The Eastern District's website has a "Representing Yourself (Pro Se Litigant)" page with a pro se packet, forms, and the Local Rules. The court also periodically hosts Pro Se Help Days at the Sacramento courthouse, where law students, professors, and volunteer attorneys offer short free consultations. Local bar lawyer referral services, legal aid organizations, and county law libraries are other general resources.
