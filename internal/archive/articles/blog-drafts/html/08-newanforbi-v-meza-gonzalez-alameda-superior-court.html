The court

The Superior Court is California's general trial court, with one in each county. A complaint is the document that starts a civil lawsuit. It identifies the parties, describes what the plaintiff says happened, and lists the legal claims, called causes of action, along with the relief requested.

The Superior Court of California, County of Alameda, serves the whole county, including Oakland, Berkeley, Hayward, Fremont, Livermore, and Dublin. According to the court's website, civil cases are heard at courthouses in Oakland (including the René C. Davidson Courthouse), Hayward, and Alameda. Like many California courts, Alameda uses "direct calendar" departments for many civil cases, meaning one judge handles a case for most purposes. Claims

The complaint is titled "Complaint for Damages." Damages are money awarded to compensate for harm. It lists three causes of action:

  • False imprisonment: under California law, the unlawful restraint of a person's freedom of movement without consent.
  • Battery: a harmful or offensive touching of another person without consent. When the claim involves a police officer, California law generally asks whether the force used was unreasonable.
  • Violation of Civil Code § 52.1 (the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act): a California statute, usually called the Bane Act, that allows a person to sue someone who interferes, or tries to interfere, by threats, intimidation, or coercion, with that person's rights under federal or state law.

It includes a demand for jury trial, which is a party's formal request that the facts be decided by a jury rather than a judge.

The named defendants are Officer Santiago Meza-Gonzalez and Officer Jose Melano, each sued individually; the State of California; and Does 1–10. Being sued individually means the claim is against the person, not just the agency. Doe defendants are placeholders that California procedure allows for people a plaintiff cannot yet identify by name; they can be substituted with real names later if identified.

The causes of action in general terms

False imprisonment. California courts generally describe the elements as a nonconsensual, intentional confinement of a person, without lawful privilege, for an appreciable period of time, however brief. Whether an officer's detention or arrest was privileged is often the central question in such a claim.

Battery by a peace officer. In an ordinary battery case, the question is whether a touching was harmful or offensive and without consent. Because officers are legally authorized to use reasonable force in some situations, California courts generally require a plaintiff suing an officer to show that the force used was unreasonable.

The Bane Act. Civil Code § 52.1 is California's state-law civil rights statute. It differs from the federal § 1983 in that it focuses on interference "by threat, intimidation, or coercion." California appellate courts have discussed how that element applies in cases involving detentions and arrests, including whether a specific intent to violate rights is required. A successful Bane Act plaintiff may recover damages and, in some cases, civil penalties and attorney's fees under the statute.

Doe defendants. Code of Civil Procedure § 474 is the provision that allows a plaintiff who does not know a defendant's name to sue that defendant by a fictitious name and amend later.

Claims involving public employees and entities, in general

California's Government Claims Act generally requires a person seeking money damages from a public entity, or in many cases from its employees for acts within the scope of employment, to first present a written claim to the entity, typically within six months for personal injury claims, before filing suit. This post describes that requirement only in general terms; the Trellis page reviewed does not address it, and this post does not address how it may apply to the Newanforbi complaint.

What the complaint alleges

The complaint describes itself as "a state tort action." A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which a court can award damages. It concerns a November 1, 2024 encounter with California Highway Patrol officers in Alameda County that, according to the complaint, began as a welfare check. Newanforbi alleges that he was detained, handcuffed, arrested, and taken to the Dublin CHP office for a blood draw, and that he was then held at Santa Rita Jail, where a parole hold extended his detention. He also alleges that no criminal charges were filed and that CHP later issued a Penal Code §§ 849/851.6 detention certificate. These are the plaintiff's allegations, not findings by the court.

For context on that last point: under California Penal Code § 849, a person who was arrested may be released without being taken before a judge in certain situations, including when officers find no grounds for a criminal complaint. Penal Code § 851.6 provides that a person released in some of those circumstances is issued a certificate describing the event as a detention rather than an arrest.

The same November 1, 2024 events are also the subject of Newanforbi's federal case, Newanforbi v. State of California (E.D. Cal. 2:25-cv-01460), which is covered in a separate post in this series. State and federal courts are separate systems, and each has its own rules for handling cases that involve overlapping events.

How to read this docket yourself

California trial court filings are generally public unless sealed or made confidential by law. Trellis is a legal research platform that collects and indexes documents from state trial courts, which is how the complaint can be read online. UniCourt is another private service that organizes California court dockets.

The official source is the Alameda County Superior Court. Its eCourt Public Portal provides online access to civil case records, including the register of actions (the state-court docket), minutes, and hearing dates; it requires a free user account, and document images can be downloaded for a fee. Because the Newanforbi case number is not shown on the Trellis page, a reader would generally start with a party-name search, then use the case number for further lookups. Civil records can also be viewed in person on public terminals at the court's civil records locations.

California dockets do not use federal "ECF" numbers. Each entry in the register of actions is listed by filing date and document title.

Representing yourself in California court

Californians may represent themselves in civil cases. Newanforbi filed this complaint in pro per. Self-represented parties are generally expected to follow the same procedural rules and deadlines as attorneys, including the Code of Civil Procedure, the California Rules of Court, and the court's local rules.

Each California superior court has a self-help center, but the types of cases each one assists with vary; Alameda's Self-Help Center, for example, states that it does not assist with general civil cases, though its website links to information about suing and being sued. The Judicial Council's online California Courts Self-Help Guide covers many civil topics. Other general resources include the Alameda County Law Library, local lawyer referral services, legal aid organizations, and attorneys who offer limited-scope representation.

Early procedure in a California civil case, in general

Under Code of Civil Procedure § 583.210, a plaintiff generally must serve the summons and complaint within three years after filing, but California Rules of Court require proof of service much sooner, generally within 60 days after the complaint is filed. A defendant considering a demurrer generally must first meet and confer with the plaintiff under § 430.41. Before a case management conference, parties typically file a Case Management Statement on Judicial Council form CM-110 at least 15 calendar days before the conference.

What happens next

In general, after a California civil complaint is filed, the plaintiff must have the summons and complaint formally delivered to each defendant, a step called service of process, and file a proof of service with the court. A defendant then usually has 30 days after service to respond, either with an answer (a written response admitting or denying the allegations) or with a demurrer, which is California's version of a motion to dismiss and challenges whether the complaint states a legally valid claim. The court also typically sets a case management conference, a meeting with the judge about the case schedule.
