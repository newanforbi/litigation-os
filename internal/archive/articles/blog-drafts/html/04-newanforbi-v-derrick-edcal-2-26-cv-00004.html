About the Eastern District of California

The Eastern District of California covers 34 counties, from the Oregon border through the Sacramento and San Joaquin Valleys to Kern County. Cases arising in counties such as Sacramento, San Joaquin, Solano, Yolo, and Placer go to the Sacramento division, and cases from counties such as Fresno, Kern, Tulare, and Stanislaus go to the Fresno division. The Newanforbi case number begins with "2:", the Sacramento designation; "26" is the year; and "DJC" and "JDP" are the initials of the assigned district judge and magistrate judge.

Under Local Rule 302(c)(21), pretrial matters in cases with a self-represented party are referred to a magistrate judge. In general, a magistrate judge can decide many non-dispositive matters directly; unless all parties have consented under 28 U.S.C. § 636(c), a recommendation to finally dismiss a case typically goes to the district judge as findings and recommendations.

What the case is about

According to the court's July 24, 2026 order, Newanforbi's first amended complaint was filed in January 2026. A complaint is the document that starts a lawsuit; a first amended complaint is a revised version that replaces the original. Under the Eastern District's Local Rule 220, an amended complaint must be complete in itself, without reference to the earlier pleading.

It brought three claims under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, the federal law that lets a person sue someone who, acting under color of state law, allegedly violated his federal rights. The claims were:

  • First Amendment retaliation, a claim that a government actor took adverse action against someone because of protected speech or activity
  • race-based disparate treatment under the Equal Protection Clause, the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee that the government will not treat people differently on the basis of race without adequate justification
  • substantive due process, a Fourteenth Amendment doctrine that protects against certain arbitrary government action

The order describes the claims as arising from a parole re-entry program that Newanforbi is required to attend. The defendants are the program's director, his parole officer, and the parole officer's supervisor (named in the order as Katie Palacios, A. Derrick, and G. Noguchi). These descriptions come from the court's summary of the complaint's allegations, not findings of fact.

The legal theories in general terms

Section 1983 and color of state law. A § 1983 plaintiff generally must show that a federal right was violated by someone acting under color of state law, meaning with authority conferred by the state. Whether a private organization counts as a state actor is decided case by case, and this post takes no position on that question in the Newanforbi case.

First Amendment retaliation. Federal courts in the Ninth Circuit generally ask whether the plaintiff engaged in protected activity, whether the defendant took action that would deter a person of ordinary firmness from continuing that activity, and whether the protected activity was a substantial or motivating factor in the defendant's action.

Equal Protection. A disparate-treatment claim generally requires facts showing that the plaintiff was treated differently from others who were similarly situated and that the defendant acted with an intent or purpose to discriminate. Government classifications based on race are subject to the most demanding level of judicial review, called strict scrutiny.

Substantive due process. Courts apply this doctrine narrowly. For conduct by executive officials, the Supreme Court has described the question as whether the conduct is so arbitrary that it "shocks the conscience."

Qualified immunity. Individual government officials sued for damages under § 1983 may raise qualified immunity, which protects officials unless they violated a clearly established right. The record reviewed does not say whether it was raised here.

July 24, 2026 order (ECF No. 31)

"ECF No." refers to the numbered entry on the court's docket. The order, posted in the Newanforbi v. Derrick court filings on the Internet Archive, made these rulings:

  • It granted Derrick and Noguchi's motion to the extent it sought dismissal of the claims, and denied it to the extent it sought to quash service. A motion to dismiss asks the court to end claims without trial, usually arguing the complaint does not state a legal claim even if its facts are assumed true. A motion to quash service challenges whether the defendant was properly served with the summons and complaint.
  • It granted Palacios's motion to dismiss.
  • It denied Newanforbi's request for email notifications, his motion for preservation of evidence and expedited discovery, and his two motions to file a supplemental opposition. It also denied a motion to strike. Discovery is the formal exchange of evidence between the parties, and "expedited" discovery means starting it earlier than the rules normally allow. A supplemental opposition is an additional brief responding to a motion. A motion to strike asks the court to remove material from a filing.
  • It dismissed the first amended complaint with leave to amend within fourteen days. If no amended complaint was filed, the action would proceed on the Equal Protection claim against Derrick.

Leave to amend is permission to file a revised complaint that tries to fix the problems the court identified. It is the opposite of a dismissal "without leave to amend," where no further revision is allowed.

The standards behind these rulings, in general

A motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim is brought under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). Under Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly (2007) and Ashcroft v. Iqbal (2009), a complaint must contain enough factual allegations to make each claim plausible; legal conclusions alone are not enough. Courts construe a self-represented party's pleadings liberally. A challenge to service is typically brought under Rule 12(b)(5). Rule 15(a)(2) directs courts to "freely give leave" to amend "when justice so requires," and courts in the Ninth Circuit generally give self-represented plaintiffs a chance to amend unless amendment would be futile.

August 13, 2026 order to show cause (ECF No. 33)

In the August 13, 2026 order on CaseMine, the court stated that Newanforbi had not filed an amended complaint. It ordered him to show cause within fourteen days why the case should not be dismissed, or to file an amended complaint. An order to show cause directs a party to explain, by a deadline, why the court should not take a particular action.

The same order noted Newanforbi's notice of voluntary dismissal of defendants Derrick and Noguchi (ECF No. 32) and directed the Clerk to terminate those defendants. A notice of voluntary dismissal is a filing in which a plaintiff drops claims against one or more defendants; in federal court, a plaintiff can generally do this without a court order before the defendant has answered or moved for summary judgment. "Terminating" a defendant means the clerk updates the docket to show that party is no longer in the case.

Orders to show cause in general

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a)(1)(A)(i) is the provision that allows a voluntary dismissal by notice. Separately, Rule 41(b) allows dismissal when a plaintiff fails to prosecute a case or comply with a court order, and courts commonly issue an order to show cause first so the party has notice and a chance to respond.

Representing yourself in federal court

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1654, a person may represent himself in federal court. Self-represented litigants must follow the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the court's Local Rules, including deadlines set in court orders. In the Eastern District, pro se litigants file on paper unless the assigned judge permits electronic filing.

The Eastern District's "Representing Yourself (Pro Se Litigant)" page offers a pro se packet, forms, and the Local Rules, and the court periodically hosts Pro Se Help Days in Sacramento with free short consultations from law students, professors, and volunteer attorneys. County law libraries and legal aid organizations are other general resources. Newanforbi's case carries the "(PS)" designation that the court uses for these self-represented matters.

What happens next

In general, after an order to show cause, the court reviews whatever the party files by the deadline, whether that is a written response, an amended complaint, or nothing. The court then decides how the case should proceed. In the Eastern District, when a magistrate judge concludes that a case should be dismissed, that recommendation usually goes to the assigned district judge in the form of findings and recommendations, which the parties may object to before the district judge rules.
