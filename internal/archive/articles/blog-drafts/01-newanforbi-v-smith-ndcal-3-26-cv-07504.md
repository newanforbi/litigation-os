## About the Northern District of California

Federal district courts are the trial courts of the federal system; California has four. The Northern District of California covers 15 counties: Alameda, Contra Costa, Del Norte, Humboldt, Lake, Marin, Mendocino, Monterey, Napa, San Benito, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, and Sonoma. It sits in San Francisco, Oakland, San Jose, and Eureka-McKinleyville.

The first digit of a Northern District case number identifies the courthouse location. A case number beginning with "3:" is a San Francisco case, "4:" is Oakland, and "5:" is San Jose. Next come the year (26), "cv" for civil, and a sequence number; the letters "LJC" are the assigned judge's initials.

The Newanforbi case was filed in the San Francisco courthouse. Under the court's Civil Local Rules, a new civil case is assigned to a courthouse based largely on the county where the dispute arose, then to a judge at random; many go directly to magistrate judges.

## The judge and the type of case

The Newanforbi case is assigned to Magistrate Judge Lisa J. Cisneros. A **magistrate judge** is a federal judicial officer appointed by the district judges of a court for a term of years. Magistrate judges handle many pretrial matters and, under 28 U.S.C. § 636(c), can preside over an entire civil case only if every party consents. If a party declines, the case is reassigned to a **district judge**, who is nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate. The pages reviewed do not show the parties' consent status.

The court lists the **nature of suit** as "Other Civil Rights" (440). Nature-of-suit codes come from the civil cover sheet filed with a new case. They are statistical categories that help courts sort cases; they do not decide what claims are actually in the case.

### How 42 U.S.C. § 1983 works

The cause of action is listed under **42 U.S.C. § 1983**, a federal law dating to 1871 that lets a person sue someone who, while acting under color of state law, allegedly violated that person's rights under the U.S. Constitution or federal law.

In general terms, a § 1983 plaintiff must allege two things: that a right secured by federal law was violated, and that the person who caused the violation was **acting under color of state law**. A private person or organization can sometimes be treated as acting under color of state law, for example when it performs a function traditionally reserved to the government or acts jointly with state officials. Courts decide that question case by case; this post takes no position on how it applies to anyone in the Newanforbi case.

Government officials sued under § 1983 often raise **qualified immunity**, a doctrine that shields them from damages unless they violated a clearly established right. The record reviewed does not describe the grounds of the defendants' motion, so it is not known whether that doctrine is at issue here.

## The procedural steps, explained

### IFP applications and screening

A plaintiff who cannot afford the federal filing fee ($405 for a civil case) can apply to proceed IFP under 28 U.S.C. § 1915. The court reviews the applicant's finances and also screens the complaint: under § 1915(e)(2), it must dismiss an IFP case at any time if, among other grounds, the complaint fails to state a claim or seeks money from a defendant who is immune. In the Newanforbi case, the docket shows IFP-related orders on July 23 and August 26, 2026.

### Pleading standards: what a motion to dismiss tests

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8 requires a complaint to contain "a short and plain statement of the claim." A motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim is brought under Rule 12(b)(6). In two Supreme Court decisions, *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (2007) and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (2009), the Court explained that a complaint must contain enough factual content to make a claim **plausible**, not merely possible. A court accepts well-pleaded factual allegations as true at this stage but does not have to accept legal conclusions. Courts read filings by self-represented parties liberally.

### Briefing and hearings in the Northern District

Under the Northern District's Civil Local Rule 7, an opposition is generally due 14 days after a motion is filed and a reply seven days after that. The Newanforbi dates (September 21, October 5, October 13) follow that pattern, with the reply moved past the October 12 federal holiday.

### Service and case management

Federal Rule 4(m) generally gives a plaintiff 90 days after filing to complete service, and Rule 12 generally gives a defendant 21 days after service to respond. The Northern District's initial scheduling order sets deadlines to meet and confer and file a joint case management statement.

## Representing yourself in federal court

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1654, parties in federal court may represent themselves. Self-represented litigants must follow the same federal and local rules as attorneys, and there is generally no right to a court-appointed lawyer in a civil case.

The Northern District maintains a "Representing Yourself" section on its website with a Pro Se Handbook, forms, and templates. It also hosts free Legal Help Centers: the Justice & Diversity Center of the Bar Association of San Francisco serves cases heard in San Francisco and Oakland, and the Asian Law Alliance's Federal Pro Se Program serves the San Jose courthouse. They offer limited-scope advice, not representation. Newanforbi appears pro se in this case.

## What happens next

At a motion hearing, the judge may hear argument, then rule from the bench or take the motion "under submission" and issue a written order later. A court deciding a motion to dismiss can grant it, deny it, or grant it in part, and when it grants a motion it may or may not allow the plaintiff to file an amended complaint ("leave to amend"). At the case management conference, the court typically discusses the schedule for the next phase of the case.
