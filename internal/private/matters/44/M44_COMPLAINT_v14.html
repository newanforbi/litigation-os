NATURE OF THE ACTION

1. Plaintiff is required to register under California Penal Code section 290. He was hired as a Night Auditor at the Delta Hotels by Marriott Santa Clara–Silicon Valley. At about 6:20 a.m. on October 29, 2026, his Night Audit trainer, Justin Howell, complained that Governor Gavin Newsom had been "mass releasing a bunch of murderer-rapists," then looked at Plaintiff and asked, "He released you didn't he?" By the time Plaintiff arrived for his next overnight shift that night, the Hotel had posted a schedule cutting him from four overnight shifts to three. On November 5, the manager, John Barros, sent a schedule cutting him to two. On November 11, Howell told Plaintiff that "John" was scheduling him less. The next day, Barros gave Plaintiff a disciplinary warning. When Plaintiff asked whether cutting his training days from four to three to two was by design, Barros answered three times: "It is by design."

2. Barros never worked an overnight shift with Plaintiff and never saw him do the Night Audit. What Barros knew about Plaintiff's overnight training and attendance, he knew from Howell. The warning Barros issued recites, to the minute, the text messages Plaintiff had sent to Howell, not to Barros. Barros signed off on the schedules, but Howell supplied the input. California law forbids any use of information disclosed through the Megan's Law website for purposes relating to employment. Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E). An employer cannot avoid liability for a biased employee's intended and effective influence by passing the decision through a manager who relies on that employee's reports. Staub v. Proctor Hosp., 562 U.S. 411, 419–22 (2011); Poland v. Chertoff, 494 F.3d 1174, 1182 (9th Cir. 2007); Reeves v. Safeway Stores, Inc., 121 Cal.App.4th 95, 113–16 (2004). Plaintiff also sues because the Hotel did not give him the standalone disclosures that federal and California law require before an employer obtains a background report.

FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

A. Hiring and the Background-Screening Packet

14. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff applied through Indeed for the Hotel's "Night Auditor / Guest Service Agent" position. On September 10, 2026, the Hotel invited him to an in-person interview with a message signed "John Barros" that read: "Upon arrival, please stop by the front desk and ask for John." Plaintiff interviewed in person at the Hotel on September 16, 2026. Barros conducted the interview.

15. On September 22, 2026, Ms. Venegas emailed Plaintiff a written offer of part-time employment at $24 per hour for Night Auditor work and $23 per hour for Guest Service Agent work, with copies to Mr. Mendez Moreno and Barros. The offer identified Barros as one of Plaintiff's supervisors. It stated that it was "contingent on the successful completion and passing of a background and reference check." The same email attached a file titled "Universal Background Check Packet 2026.pdf" and stated: "Once I receive the signed authorization, you will receive an email from Universal Background Screening to complete the background check process."

16. On September 22, 2026, Plaintiff returned his signed offer and "The completed and signed Background Check Authorization form." On September 24, Ms. Venegas replied, with copies to Mr. Mendez Moreno and Barros: "We are super excited to bring you on board. Could you please let me know when you will be able to start?" Plaintiff began work on or about September 25, 2026.

17. The packet contained three screening pages. The first was a "Criminal Background Checking Policy" on "Delta Santa Clara" letterhead. It stated that "the applicant's first day of work in the position must not be prior to the satisfactory completion of the criminal conviction check," and it bore the line "Source: Society for Human Resource Management" and a statement that it "is not intended as legal advice." The second was an "Acknowledgment and Authorization for Background Investigation." It stated: "I acknowledge receipt of the separate documents entitled DISCLOSURE REGARDING BACKGROUND INVESTIGATION, DISCLOSURE FOR INVESTIGATIVE CONSUMER REPORT (if applicable), A SUMMARY OF YOUR RIGHTS UNDER THE FAIR CREDIT REPORTING ACT." It also authorized "consumer reports" and "investigative consumer reports" by "Delta Santa Clara" through Universal Background Screening. The third was a "Background Check Information" form collecting identifying information for the consumer reporting agency.

18. The packet did not include any document titled "DISCLOSURE REGARDING BACKGROUND INVESTIGATION" or "DISCLOSURE FOR INVESTIGATIVE CONSUMER REPORT." Plaintiff was never given those documents, or any other standalone disclosure, through any channel. The packet contained no box or other means to request a copy of any report. Plaintiff never received any communication from Universal Background Screening or any other background-check company. The Policy cannot serve as the disclosure: it contains a source attribution, a legal disclaimer, and rules on convictions and the first day of work, and it was sent with the offer and onboarding materials.

19. On information and belief, HMG procured, or caused to be procured, a consumer report and investigative consumer report concerning Plaintiff from Universal Background Screening for employment purposes. The basis for that belief is that the forms are on HMG's trade name, "Delta Santa Clara"; the offer was contingent on a background check; the Policy forbade a first day of work before the check was complete; Ms. Venegas wrote that Universal Background Screening would contact Plaintiff after she received his authorization; two days after he returned it, she asked when he could start; and he then began work. Plaintiff did not receive a copy of any report.

20. The order records, the report, and the end-user certification are in Defendants' and Universal Background Screening's possession.

21. Plaintiff understood that the Hotel intended to run a background check. But nothing he received told him that the Hotel would obtain an investigative consumer report; what sources would be searched or what the report would contain; who would receive it; that the authorization covered reports "throughout [his] employment" and released "any and all background information" "without reservation"; or that he could obtain a free copy. Had he received a clear, standalone disclosure, he would not have signed an authorization covering his entire employment "without reservation"; he would have asked what would be searched and requested a copy of any report. Defendants obtained Plaintiff's personal information without the disclosure the law requires, invading his interest in controlling the use of that information for employment decisions. He never received any disclosure at all; this is not a complaint about format. See Syed v. M-I, LLC, 853 F.3d 492, 499–500 (9th Cir. 2017); TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413, 425, 440–42 (2021).

25. Barros never worked an overnight shift with Plaintiff and never observed Plaintiff perform Night Audit work. He had no firsthand knowledge of Plaintiff's performance, progress, or attendance on the overnight shifts. On information and belief, Barros's knowledge of Plaintiff's Night Audit training and overnight attendance came solely from Howell's reports. The basis for that belief is that Barros did not work overnights; Howell was the trainer assigned to assess Plaintiff and report to Barros, and the only person who had observed Plaintiff perform the Night Audit (¶ 24); Plaintiff sent his overnight attendance notices to Howell (¶ 37); and Barros's November 3 text shows that he received information about Plaintiff's overnight attendance from someone other than Plaintiff (¶ 38).

E. How Howell Learned of Plaintiff's Registration

30. Plaintiff is required to register under Penal Code section 290. On information and belief, throughout his employment at the Hotel, the California Department of Justice displayed information about Plaintiff on its Megan's Law website under Penal Code section 290.46, including his name, photograph, and the nature of his registrable offense. The basis for that belief is that Plaintiff's profile is publicly posted on the website, and his registration status did not change between September and November 2026. Plaintiff has not viewed the listing himself. The website allows any member of the public to search for a registrant by name. Information from it is also republished by other websites.

31. Plaintiff never told Howell, Barros, or anyone else at the Hotel about his registration or his offense, at any time, including during the hiring process. Plaintiff wore an ankle monitor that at times bulged visibly through the leg of his dress pants. Coworkers, including Howell, looked at it. A visible monitor signals that a person is under some form of supervision. It does not reveal why, and it does not reveal the nature of any offense. It did, however, give a coworker reason to wonder, and a reason to look Plaintiff up.

32. On information and belief, Howell learned of Plaintiff's registrant status and the nature of his offense by searching Plaintiff's name online and viewing Plaintiff's Megan's Law listing, or information republished from it. The basis for that belief is as follows. Plaintiff's full name, which is distinctive, appeared on the posted rosters and on the work computer after he logged in, so Howell had what he needed to search by name. The front-desk computers Howell used on overnight shifts had internet access, and Plaintiff saw Howell browsing the internet on them during overnight shifts. Howell's remark was specific to released sex offenders ("murderer-rapists"), and the Megan's Law website is the public source that identifies a named person as a sex offender and describes the offense. Plaintiff had disclosed that information to no one at the Hotel. And the visible monitor gave Howell a reason to look. [If a search of Plaintiff's full name on an ordinary search engine returns his registry information, state that here.] The Hotel's browser histories and its DNS, proxy, and firewall logs for the front-desk computers will show which employees searched Plaintiff's name and what they viewed. Those records are in Defendants' possession.

33. In the alternative, and also on information and belief, information disclosed under section 290.46 reached the Hotel through the background investigation alleged in paragraph 19. The Hotel obtained a criminal background check on Plaintiff as a condition of employment, and employment-screening businesses have accessed the Megan's Law website in screening applicants. See Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Servs., Inc., 182 Cal.App.4th 1644 (2010). The sources searched and the recipients of any report are in Defendants' and Universal Background Screening's possession.

F. Howell's Selective Fixation on Attendance

34. Howell is preoccupied with his coworkers' attendance. During down time on the overnight shifts he and Plaintiff worked together, Howell regularly volunteered, unprompted, his opinions about what was wrong with management and with his coworkers. On several of those shifts he told Plaintiff that the Lucas Lounge employees who staff the Hotel's coffee and food stations are "routinely late by at least 30 minutes every day," and complained that management was not doing anything about it. In the same conversations, he told Plaintiff, "If I were management I would fire them all and re-hire under stricter rules."

35. Plaintiff does not know whether those employees were later disciplined. Howell's own repeated complaint, however, was that management was not disciplining them or enforcing attendance against them.

36. By Howell's own account, management did not act on the daily lateness of other employees. Plaintiff's attendance, by contrast, led to two cuts in his shifts. Both cuts came before Plaintiff received any discipline at all, and the first came after Howell's October 29 remark identifying Plaintiff as one of the released "murderer-rapists."

37. Plaintiff sent his overnight attendance notices to Howell. Plaintiff did not call or text Thao Truong. On November 3 at 11:05 p.m. and November 4 at 11:21 p.m., he texted Howell that he was on his way.

Plaintiff had reported the illness to Howell and to no one else at the Hotel. Thao Truong, who was working that night, was not told why Plaintiff was absent. On information and belief, Barros heard it from Howell. The warning Barros issued nine days later confirms that Barros had the details of Plaintiff's text to Howell (¶ 49B).

39. On information and belief, between October 13 and November 12, 2026, Howell sent Barros text messages and emails, and spoke with him, giving negative reports about Plaintiff's performance in Night Audit training and about his attendance, including the attendance notices Plaintiff had sent only to Howell. On information and belief, Howell recommended that Barros reduce Plaintiff's overnight shifts because of Plaintiff's call-outs and late arrivals. On November 11, Howell confirmed to Plaintiff, firsthand, that Barros was cutting Plaintiff's schedule and that attendance was the reason: "Well, it's because you've been calling out of your shifts. That's why John has been scheduling you less and less." (¶ 48). On information and belief, Howell's hostility toward Plaintiff's registrant status shaped his reports and his recommendation. He used them to advocate for and bring about the reduction of Plaintiff's shifts from four per week to three and then to two, and ultimately the warning issued on November 12, 2026. The messages are in Defendants' and Howell's possession.

40. The basis for the beliefs alleged in paragraph 39 is as follows:

(a) Howell was Plaintiff's assigned trainer and evaluator, responsible for reporting to Barros, and the only person who had Plaintiff perform the Night Audit (¶ 24). Barros had no other source of information about Plaintiff's overnight work (¶ 25).

(b) Howell threatened to report Plaintiff's supposed "incompetence" to "John" and questioned why John kept spending time and money training Plaintiff (¶ 26).

(c) Howell's October 29 remark revealed animus toward Plaintiff's registrant status (¶ 28).

(d) Plaintiff sent his overnight attendance notices to Howell (¶ 37), and Barros then learned of the November 2 absence from someone other than Plaintiff (¶ 38).

(e) Howell is fixated on coworkers' attendance and favors cutting and firing employees with attendance problems (¶ 34).

(f) On November 11, Howell told Plaintiff that John was scheduling him less because of his call-outs (¶ 48). The explanation Howell gave was the attendance theme Howell himself pressed, and it shows that Howell knew Barros's reasons.

(g) Howell reaffirmed on November 10 that he was "still [Plaintiff's] trainer" (¶ 26).

(h) Hotel management relies on Howell's judgment about Night Audit (¶ 27).

(i) Each reduction followed Howell's October 29 remark and Plaintiff's attendance notices to Howell (¶¶ 43–47).

(j) Barros's November 12 warning recites, with their exact times, the texts Plaintiff sent to Howell, and not to Barros, on November 2 (11:01 p.m.), November 3 (11:05 p.m.), and November 4 (11:21 p.m.). It also states that Plaintiff's attendance "impacted the team's ability to provide consistent training," the training Howell provided (¶ 49B).

41. On information and belief, Howell intended his reports and recommendation to cause Barros to cut Plaintiff's Night Audit shifts and to discipline him. The basis for that belief is his threat to report Plaintiff to John; his complaint that John was wasting time and money training Plaintiff; his October 29 remark; his continued hovering over Plaintiff's work; his view that employees with attendance problems should be fired; and the fact that the reductions he later described to Plaintiff were the result his reports were designed to produce.

H. Barros, Without Firsthand Knowledge, Acted on Howell's Input

42. On information and belief, Barros himself harbored no animus toward Plaintiff's registrant status. Barros relied both on Howell's evaluation of Plaintiff's training and on Howell's opinions about Plaintiff's attendance in deciding to reduce Plaintiff's shifts and to issue the warning. The basis for that belief is that Barros had no firsthand knowledge of Plaintiff's overnight work (¶ 25); that he acted on what he had "heard" (¶ 38); and that the reason Howell gave Plaintiff for the reductions, call-outs, is the reason Howell had urged (¶¶ 39–40). The warning itself rests in part on attendance information that came from Howell (¶ 49B). Barros thus served as the conduit through which Howell's bias produced the adverse actions. Reeves, 121 Cal.App.4th at 113–14.

I. The Reductions and the Warning

43. The first reduction was decided within a day of Howell's October 29 remark. The November 1–7 schedule, which cut Plaintiff from four overnights to three, was already posted on the clipboard in the front desk office when Plaintiff arrived for his October 29 overnight shift. Plaintiff saw it when he arrived and photographed it before he left at 7:03 a.m. on October 30. Barros, who does not work overnights, therefore set that schedule after Howell's remark that morning and before Plaintiff's October 29 overnight shift. On information and belief, Howell's reports were a cause of the reduction. The basis for that belief is the timing, Howell's role and statements, and the facts alleged in paragraph 40.

44. The first cut was already posted when, at 10:55 a.m. that same morning, Plaintiff reported to Barros that he could perform about 70 percent of Night Audit tasks without Howell's help and offered to cover front-desk shifts on November 5 or 6 (¶ 23). Barros made no change. When Barros wrote that evening that he would "reach out if occupancy changes and additional staff is needed," the posted schedule had already given those very days' front-desk shifts to others. On November 5, Night Auditor Thao Truong was scheduled at the front desk from 7:00 a.m. to 3:30 p.m., and newly hired trainee Miguel Bretado from 3:00 p.m. to 11:00 p.m. On November 6, Thao Truong was again scheduled at the front desk.

45. Before the reductions, Barros had given Plaintiff two attendance reminders. On October 8, after Plaintiff emailed Ms. Venegas to call out of a training shift, Barros texted that Plaintiff "should always contact me." On October 27, after the start of an overnight training shift, Plaintiff texted Barros that he could not attend that night's training and would be there the next evening; on October 28 Barros replied with the Hotel's four-hour notice policy. Plaintiff acknowledged each reminder. Neither reminder said that Plaintiff's hours would be reduced.

46. On Thursday, November 5, 2026, at 1:59 p.m., Barros texted Plaintiff that he had "just checked your time card and you were late 37 minutes," without identifying the shift. The November 12 warning later identified it as Plaintiff's November 4 shift. Plaintiff apologized. At 5:52 p.m. that day, Barros sent Plaintiff the November 8–14 schedule, which cut him to two overnights, November 10 and 11, both alongside Howell. That schedule assigned Howell to five overnights and gave Thao Truong and trainee Miguel Bretado front-desk shifts on November 12, 13, and 14, days on which Plaintiff was not scheduled at all.

47. The second reduction followed Plaintiff's November 2 illness notice to Howell, Barros's November 3 text that he had "heard" about it from someone else, and Plaintiff's November 3 and 4 notices to Howell. On information and belief, Howell's reports and recommendation were a cause of the second reduction, on the basis alleged in paragraph 40.

48. At about 6:30 a.m. on November 11, 2026, on the overnight shift that began November 10, Plaintiff raised with Howell that he was being scheduled for fewer days. Howell told Plaintiff, in substance: "Well, it's because you've been calling out of your shifts. That's why John has been scheduling you less and less."

49. On November 12, 2026, the day after that conversation, Barros met with Plaintiff and issued him an "Employee Disciplinary Action Form" dated November 12, 2026, which Barros and Plaintiff signed and of which Barros kept the signed copies. FIRST CAUSE OF ACTION

Unlawful Use of Megan's Law Information for Employment Purposes,

Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j), Including Through Cat's-Paw Imputation

(Against All Defendants)

57. Plaintiff incorporates paragraphs 1 through 56.

58. Penal Code section 290.46(j)(1) provides that "[a] person is authorized to use information disclosed pursuant to this section only to protect a person at risk." Section 290.46(j)(2) provides that, except as authorized under paragraph (1) or any other provision of law, "use of any information that is disclosed pursuant to this section for purposes relating to any of the following is prohibited," including "(E) Employment." Under section 290.46(j)(4)(A), "[a]ny use of information disclosed pursuant to this section for purposes other than those provided by paragraph (1) or in violation of paragraph (2) shall make the user liable for the actual damages, and any amount that may be determined by a jury or a court sitting without a jury, not exceeding three times the amount of actual damage, and not less than two hundred fifty dollars ($250), and attorney's fees, exemplary damages, or a civil penalty not exceeding twenty-five thousand dollars ($25,000)." An employer that uses Megan's Law website information as a basis for an employment decision is liable. Mendoza, 182 Cal.App.4th at 1657.

59. On information and belief, information about Plaintiff was disclosed pursuant to section 290.46 on the Megan's Law website (¶ 30), and it reached the Hotel through Howell's viewing of the website (¶ 32) or through the background investigation (¶ 33).

60. Howell used that information for purposes relating to Plaintiff's employment. Acting in his assigned role as Plaintiff's trainer and evaluator, he let it shape his assessment of Plaintiff and his reports and recommendation to Barros. He used them to advocate for cutting Plaintiff's shifts and disciplining him (¶¶ 39–41). Barros, who had no firsthand knowledge of Plaintiff's overnight work, acted on Howell's input in reducing Plaintiff's overnight shifts from four to three and then to two, and in issuing the November 12 warning (¶¶ 42–49). The Hotel thereby used the information as a basis for employment decisions. No Defendant used the information to protect a person at risk: the Hotel kept Plaintiff working overnight with guests and staff. On information and belief, no Defendant obtained or used the information under any authority listed in section 290.46(j)(3).

61. Defendants are liable for that use under the cat's-paw rule. When a biased employee performs an act motivated by animus that he intends to cause an adverse employment action, and that act is a proximate cause of the action, the employer is liable even though another person made the final decision. Staub, 562 U.S. at 419–22. The biased employee's animus is imputed to the employer when he "influenced or was involved in the decision or decisionmaking process." Poland, 494 F.3d at 1182; France v. Johnson, 795 F.3d 1170, 1176 (9th Cir. 2015). California follows the same principle. Reeves, 121 Cal.App.4th at 113–16; see McGrory v. Applied Signal Tech., Inc., 212 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1536 (2013). Each element is pleaded:

(a) animus based on Plaintiff's registrant status (¶¶ 28–29);

(b) a biased act, namely Howell's negative reports and his recommendation to cut Plaintiff's shifts (¶¶ 39–40);

(c) Howell's intent to cause the reductions and discipline (¶ 41);

(d) an evaluator whose input the decisionmaker depended on, because the decisionmaker had no firsthand knowledge of Plaintiff's overnight work (¶¶ 24–25, 42);

(e) proximate cause: the reductions and the warning followed Howell's reports and were based on them, and the stated attendance reason was pretextual and selectively applied (¶¶ 43–52); and

(f) no truly independent investigation by Barros (¶ 53).

62. Howell acted within the course and scope of his employment and agency when he trained, evaluated, and reported on Plaintiff, because the Hotel assigned him those duties (¶ 24). Barros acted within the course and scope of his employment and agency when he set Plaintiff's schedule and issued discipline. HMG is liable for their acts. Cal. Civ. Code § 2338. Harrell is liable as joint employer, integrated enterprise, and principal (¶ 5). In the alternative, Defendants are liable for Barros's own use of the information (¶ 54).

63. As a direct and proximate result, Plaintiff lost paid shifts and wages, received a written warning, and suffered emotional distress (¶¶ 55–56). Howell's use of Plaintiff's registrant status to cut his work was intentional and done in conscious disregard of Plaintiff's rights, which supports exemplary damages or a civil penalty. Plaintiff is entitled to the relief provided by section 290.46(j)(4)(A).
