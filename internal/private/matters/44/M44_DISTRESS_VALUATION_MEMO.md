# Matter 44 — Emotional-distress pleading and valuation (PRIVATE; not for the complaint)

Prepared Nov. 17, 2026 at Plaintiff's request, as a thinking aid. It is not a settlement figure, demand, or valuation for any outside party. Every symptom pleaded or testified to must be true. Each will be tested at deposition, and possibly by a court-ordered mental examination.

## 1. How distress is proved and what it costs to claim it

| Tier | What it looks like | Proof | Litigation cost to Plaintiff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. "Garden variety"** | Humiliation, worry, anger, sleeplessness, stress over money and housing. No diagnosis, no treatment. | Plaintiff's own testimony. Lay testimony alone can support an award (*Newton v. Equilon*, 411 F.Supp.3d 856, 877 (N.D. Cal. 2019), per the Nov. 11 Westlaw findings). | Lowest. Courts generally do not order a Rule 35 mental exam or open mental-health records for garden-variety claims (practice varies by judge). |
| **B. Corroborated** | Tier A plus observable effects: weight loss, withdrawal, panic about eviction, effects lasting weeks or months. | Plaintiff, plus a family member, friend, or roommate who saw it. Texts and journals from the time. | Moderate. Witnesses get deposed. |
| **C. Clinical** | A diagnosed condition (e.g., an anxiety or adjustment disorder), treatment, medication, and continuing or future distress. | Treating provider records and testimony; often a retained expert. | Highest. It puts mental condition "in controversy." Expect a **Rule 35 examination** and production of treatment records. The patient-litigant exception (Cal. Evid. Code § 1016) applies to the state claims. In federal court the psychotherapist privilege (*Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1 (1996)) may be found waived. The defense will explore your full history, including incarceration and supervision. |

**Recommendation:** plead Tier A, made concrete by the housing facts (¶¶ 56A–56C), and add Tier B witnesses if they exist. Move to Tier C only if Plaintiff actually seeks treatment. Never claim a diagnosis that has not been made.

## 2. Hypothetical: the highest-value distress narrative the law would recognize

*This is a hypothetical to show what the strongest truthful pleading would contain. These are not Plaintiff's facts. Use only what actually happened.*

> After the October 30 cut, the plaintiff began waking at night calculating whether his pay would cover the next week's rent. When the second cut posted on November 5, he stopped sleeping more than a few hours a night. He lost weight, and he drained most of his savings over the following weeks. He knew that losing his housing would force him to register as transient, with more frequent in-person registration and a greater risk of a technical violation [verify the transient-registration rules, Pen. Code § 290.011], so he lived with daily fear of falling out of compliance. He described the hotel's treatment, being singled out as one of the "murderer-rapists" and then cut, as the most humiliating experience since his release, and avoided coworkers out of shame. His sister and roommate saw him withdraw and stop eating. He sought counseling, was diagnosed with an adjustment disorder with anxiety, and was still in treatment at trial, with a provider testifying that the distress would continue for at least a year.

What makes that hypothetical "max value":
1. **Duration and continuity.** It starts at the first cut, worsens at the second, and is still present at trial.
2. **Specific physical effects.** Sleep, weight, appetite.
3. **A concrete, foreseeable stressor tied to the adverse action:** rent shortfall, depleted savings, and housing loss.
4. **A harm unique to the plaintiff that makes the stakes higher than for an ordinary employee.** For a registrant, housing loss means transient registration and compliance risk. Plead it **only if Plaintiff actually experienced that fear.**
5. **Dignitary harm tied to the protected information.** He was humiliated because of the registry status, which is exactly what § 290.46 protects against.
6. **Corroboration** by people who saw it.
7. **Treatment and a professional opinion** about future distress.

## 3. Valuation reference points (from the Nov. 11 Westlaw findings; not predictions)

- *Newton v. Equilon* (N.D. Cal. 2019): $475,000 past and future distress on lay testimony plus coworkers. FEHA harassment; inflammatory facts.
- *Colucci v. T-Mobile* (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 442: $500,000 past plus $200,000 future, with a psychologist. Termination case.
- *McCray v. Westrock* (C.D. Cal. 2024): $1.5M past plus $1M future, with an expert and a spouse. Termination case.
- *Pierce v. East Bay MUD* (9th Cir. 2026, unpublished memorandum): $2M non-economic affirmed. The closest non-termination comparator, but it is not precedent.

**Discounts that apply here:** Plaintiff was not fired (hours reduced and partly restored); the case is small-dollar on wages; the attendance record will color a jury; there is no reported § 290.46 damages award; and the employer will argue workers' compensation exclusivity (*Miklosy*) against distress damages. That argument is weaker for a statutory violation, but it is untested for § 290.46.

## 4. The § 290.46 multiplier (unsettled; plan around it, don't count on it)

Section 290.46(j)(4)(A) awards **actual damages**, plus an additional amount "not exceeding three times the amount of actual damage" (minimum $250), plus exemplary damages **or** a civil penalty up to $25,000.

- If emotional distress counts as "actual damage" (the statute doesn't exclude it, and tort "actual damages" usually include it), a distress award of D could support up to D + 3D, plus the penalty.
- Illustration only: proven distress of $50,000 plus $800 in wages would support up to about $203,200, plus up to $25,000. Whether the multiplier reaches distress, and whether a jury would award it, is undecided. **No reported case applies it.**
- FCRA actual damages can include distress but must flow from the disclosure violation, which is a weak causal link here. ICRAA's floor is $10,000 per report.

## 5. Bottom line for drafting

- Plead truthful Tier A facts with the housing math. They are concrete, sympathetic, and cheap to prove.
- Record a dated memo-to-file now, in Plaintiff's own words, describing how he has been affected and when it started. Memory fades, and the memo becomes your own contemporaneous record.
- Identify any witness who saw the effects.
- Decide on Tier C only if treatment actually happens. Weigh the Rule 35 exam and the history discovery before claiming it.
