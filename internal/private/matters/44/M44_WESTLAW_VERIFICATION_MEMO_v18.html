1.2 · Connor proposition · ¶ 70 · CHECK

v18 says: "A criminal-history report obtained for employment is an investigative consumer report. See Connor v. First Student, Inc., 5 Cal.5th 1026 (2018)." That may overstate Connor. As I understand it, Connor held that ICRAA is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to employer background checks that overlap with the CCRAA. It did not hold that every criminal-history report is investigative. Record:

  • The pin where Connor describes the background checks at issue and holds that ICRAA applies to them.
  • The definition of "investigative consumer report" in § 1786.2(c). Does it cover information on character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living "obtained through any means"?

If Connor doesn't say it flatly, v19 will read: "ICRAA applies to employment background checks that report criminal history. See Connor, 5 Cal.5th at ___; Cal. Civ. Code § 1786.2(c)."

3.1 Marriott's motion to dismiss (most likely first attack)

(a) Who can be liable for "causing" a report to be procured?

Look for any case holding a non-employer liable under § 1681b(b)(2) because it caused the report: a parent company, franchisor, staffing client, or brand that required the check. Look also for cases confining liability to the entity that actually ordered it.

"cause! a consumer report to be procured" /p (franchis! parent client brand) 1681b(b)(2) /p (franchisor franchisee) /p "background check" ICRAA 1786.16 /p "cause! to be prepared" /p (franchis! parent client)

Record: what the plaintiff alleged about the non-employer's role. Was it a requirement, a vendor contract, a certification, or access to systems? Did it survive dismissal? This tells us whether ¶ 21C is enough as pleaded or needs the UBS file first.

(b) Agency law for the FCRA count is federal, not California

¶ 21E cites only Civ. Code §§ 2300, 2317, and 2334. That works for ICRAA. For the FCRA count, courts generally apply federal common-law agency principles, so Marriott will argue the California citations don't reach it. Leads to read:

  • LEAD [UNVERIFIED] Meyer v. Holley, 537 U.S. 280 (2003). Federal statutory torts incorporate traditional vicarious-liability rules. It's a Fair Housing Act case, so check the pin for that principle.
  • LEAD [UNVERIFIED] Jones v. Federated Fin. Reserve Corp., 144 F.3d 961 (6th Cir. 1998). FCRA liability for obtaining a report may be imposed through agency principles, including apparent authority. Check the Ninth Circuit's treatment.
  • Restatement (Third) of Agency §§ 2.03 (apparent authority) and 7.08 (liability for an agent acting with apparent authority).
