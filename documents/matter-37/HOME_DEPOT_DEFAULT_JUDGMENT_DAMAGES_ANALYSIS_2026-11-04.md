# Default Judgment Damages Analysis — Strongest Plaintiff-Side Case Under FRCP 55(b)(2)

**Case:** *Brendan Ngwa Nforbi v. Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.*, No. 2:26-cv-03310-TLN-AC (E.D. Cal.)
**Claim:** California Penal Code § 290.46(j) — unlawful use of Megan's Law registry information in an employment decision; remedy under § 290.46(j)(4)(A).
**Posture assumed:** Marshal service on registered agent October 14, 2026; answer due November 4, 2026; default entered under Rule 55(a) (assumed for this analysis).
**Standard:** On default, the well-pleaded allegations of the complaint are deemed admitted. *TeleVideo Sys. v. Heidenthal*, 826 F.2d 915 (9th Cir. 1987).

> **Posture flag — read first.** This analysis assumes Rule 55(a) default has been entered. As of November 4, 2026, the answer is due that day, so default is not yet available. Do not file this motion until the clerk actually enters default.

## The statute as written — two corrections that change the math

Section 290.46(j)(4)(A) makes the user "liable for the actual damages, **and** any amount that may be determined by a jury or a court sitting without a jury, **not exceeding three times the amount of actual damage**, and not less than two hundred fifty dollars ($250), and attorney's fees, **exemplary damages, or a civil penalty not exceeding twenty-five thousand dollars ($25,000)**."

Two consequences follow. **First, the 4× total (actual damages plus up to 3× additional) is not the aggressive reading — it is the textual one.** The phrase "not exceeding three times the amount of actual damage" modifies the additional amount, not the total. The 3×-total reading is the fallback, not the lead. **Second, exemplary damages and the $25,000 civil penalty are disjunctive:** plaintiff elects one and does not stack both. The attorney's-fees clause does not help a pro se plaintiff — *Trope v. Katz* bars fees for a party's own time — but costs are recoverable.

## 1. Rule 54(c) notice — why "according to proof" plus the statutory prayer covers this size

Rule 54(c) provides that a default judgment "must not differ in kind from, or exceed in amount, what is demanded in the pleadings." Its purpose is fair notice, not a cap on proof. The prayer here demands (a) actual damages according to proof, (b) statutory damages under (j)(4)(A), (c) exemplary damages, (d) costs, and (e) pre- and post-judgment interest, and the complaint pleads an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000. The argument: the prayer incorporates the statutory formula itself — actuals plus up to 3× additional plus exemplary — so the ceiling was computable from the face of the complaint on the day Home Depot was served. Home Depot, a national employer with counsel, could do the same arithmetic: wage rate × weeks × 4. "According to proof" does not mean "unlimited"; it means "the amount the proof establishes under the demanded measure," and the demanded measure here is a statute with a built-in multiplier. Add the liberal construction owed to a pro se pleading, and the notice argument is strong. The risk is real and is addressed as Counterargument 1 below.

## 2. The multiplier — why full trebling, and why willfulness is established on default

The additional amount is discretionary ("not exceeding" 3×), so plaintiff must earn the ceiling. Three points do it. First, the violation is admitted: on default the well-pleaded allegations are deemed admitted (*TeleVideo*), and the admitted facts are that Home Depot ran a background check and then halted onboarding using registry information — the precise use subdivision (j)(2) forbids. Second, this was not an accident available to an ordinary employer: a national retailer running a screening program that pulls registry information and then acts on it is acting knowingly, and default forfeits any innocent-mistake explanation Home Depot might have offered. Third, the statute's structure rewards the ceiling: the $250 floor exists for cases with trivial actuals; where actuals are substantial and the violation is admitted and unexplained, anything less than the ceiling under-deters the exact conduct the statute targets. Discretion cuts both ways, and there is nothing in this record — no excuse, no explanation, no appearance at all — pulling toward leniency.

## 3. The April 2028 end date, and front pay to retirement

Be precise about what April 15, 2028 is, because a court will be. Back pay accrues from the adverse action until judgment; it cannot, strictly, include weeks that have not happened yet. The honest frame — and the one that preserves the total — is this: the $109,989.29 figure is the full period of wage loss claimed at $575/week, and the court should fix the cutoff at the actual judgment date, treating everything after judgment as front pay at the same rate rather than striking it. That recharacterization costs nothing if the court accepts the weekly rate and the loss period; it only changes the label and requires discounting of the post-judgment portion.

Front pay to retirement is the aggressive half, and it needs its own theory, because "he would have worked there until 67" will not survive contact with an at-will, part-time warehouse job. The theory that can carry it: the registry record is permanent, and the exclusion is not a one-employer event. Every future employer in warehousing and logistics that runs a background check will see the same record and can make the same decision — the unlawful decision did not just cost one job, it marked plaintiff in the labor market for this sector. That is why lost future earnings "flow from" the decision and are recoverable as actual damages rather than speculation: the mechanism of future loss is the same registry information, still in circulation. Support it with (a) proof the record remains publicly available, (b) evidence of the sector's near-universal use of background screening, and (c) plaintiff's own post-2024 job-search history showing repeated rejections or screening failures.

Age, years to retirement, and discount rate were left blank in the instructions and are not invented here. The formula is: front pay = $29,900/year × N years to age 67, discounted to present value at rate r, then included in actuals and trebled with everything else. For scale only — not plaintiff's figures — at a 3% discount rate:

| Horizon | Present value at 3% |
|---|---|
| 5 years | ≈ $136,900 |
| 10 years | ≈ $255,100 |
| 20 years | ≈ $444,800 |
| 30 years | ≈ $586,100 |

Fill in N and r from the declaration and an economist or a published work-life table, or the court will fill them in against plaintiff.

## 4. Prejudgment interest under Civil Code § 3288 at 7%

Section 3288 permits interest in the court's discretion in cases of oppression, fraud, or malice — the same showing that supports exemplary damages, at lower stakes. The admitted knowing use of registry information to kill an onboarding, unexplained and undefended, is oppression in the § 3288 sense: a statutory violation committed against a jobseeker with no bargaining power and no explanation. The 7% rate is the constitutional default where no rate is specified, and simple interest accruing per pay period (not on the whole sum from day one, and not on the treble or exemplary portions) is the conservative, defensible computation — which is exactly how the $14,132.87 figure is built. Ask for interest on actuals only, recomputed to the actual judgment date, and the court has no reason to cut it.

## 5. Exemplary damages under Civil Code § 3294

Elect exemplary damages and drop the $25,000 civil penalty — the statute gives one or the other. The § 3294 showing is oppression, fraud, or malice by clear and convincing evidence. Plaintiff's case: a sophisticated national employer used a statutorily forbidden information source to deny employment, gave no reason, and then defaulted rather than explain itself. That is conscious disregard of a known legal prohibition and of plaintiff's rights — malice in the § 3294 sense, not mere negligence. Two gaps must be closed with evidence, because the admitted facts alone do not close them: (a) corporate ratification — § 3294(b) requires an officer, director, or managing agent's act or ratification, so the declaration must tie the screening-and-halt decision to a managing agent or a corporate screening policy, not to an anonymous clerk; (b) financial condition — *Adams v. Murakami* requires evidence of the defendant's financial condition before a punitive award, so put Home Depot's public financials (Form 10-K, revenues) in the record rather than asking the court to notice that Home Depot is big. Keep the exemplary ask modest relative to the trebled total — at or below 1× actuals — so the *State Farm v. Campbell* single-digit ratio guidepost is satisfied and the "double punishment" objection (treble plus punitive) loses its force: the treble is statutory compensation and deterrence set by the Legislature, the exemplary is the additional amount the statute separately authorizes for this conduct.

## 6. The *Eitel* factors, in plaintiff's favor

*Eitel v. McCool*, 782 F.2d 1470 (9th Cir. 1986):

- **(1) Prejudice to plaintiff** — without default judgment, an admitted statutory violation goes unremedied and the wage loss continues; favors plaintiff.
- **(2) Merits** — the admitted facts plead every element of (j)(2)/(j)(4)(A): use of registry information in an employment decision causing loss; favors plaintiff.
- **(3) Sufficiency of the complaint** — the single-count complaint tracks the statute; the court still reviews sufficiency on default, but there is no pleading defect to find; favors plaintiff.
- **(4) Sum at stake** — the only factor that cuts against plaintiff, because the total is large; answer it with the statute itself, which sets the multiplier, so the size is the Legislature's judgment, not plaintiff's invention.
- **(5) Possibility of factual dispute** — none material remains, because the facts are admitted and Home Depot offered no competing version; favors plaintiff.
- **(6) Excusable neglect** — a Fortune-scale corporation served through its registered agent, with counsel available, that simply never appeared; nothing excusable on this record; favors plaintiff.
- **(7) Policy favoring decisions on the merits** — the only other soft factor, but it is routinely overcome where the defendant willfully defaults, which is this case; neutral to slightly against, and outweighed by the other six.

## 7. What proves each line — declaration and exhibit checklist

- **Liability packet:** the complaint, the Marshal's return of service (Oct. 14, 2026, CSC Sacramento), the clerk's Rule 55(a) entry of default, and a short brief tying the admitted facts to (j)(2) and (j)(4)(A).
- **Wage rate and hours:** the offer letter or onboarding paperwork showing the Tracy warehouse position, $23/hr, 25 hrs/week; if the paper is missing, a declaration attesting to the rate and schedule plus any email or portal screenshot showing it.
- **Back-pay period:** a declaration stating the hire, acceptance, and onboarding dates, the halt with no reason given, and continued unemployment or underemployment since — with a week-by-week computation table at $575/week from Aug. 15, 2024 to the judgment date, and the April 2028 figure shown as the claimed outer bound with post-judgment weeks separately identified for front-pay treatment.
- **Mitigation:** a job-search log (applications, dates, outcomes) and any interim earnings records, with interim earnings deducted week by week. This is the exhibit most pro se default movants skip and most courts ask for first.
- **Front pay:** the registry record as it stands today (proving persistence), evidence of sector screening practices, the job-search history, and either an economist's declaration or a published work-life table supplying N and r. Without these, front pay is the first thing cut.
- **Interest:** a per-period interest schedule at 7% simple on actuals only, recomputed to judgment.
- **Exemplary:** a managing-agent/policy showing, Home Depot's public financials, and a short statement of why the amount requested satisfies *Campbell*.
- **Costs:** the bill of costs — filing fee, the Marshal service cost ($69.41: $65.00 + $4.41 mileage, per the USM-285), and any other taxable costs under 28 U.S.C. § 1920.

## 8. The numbers: highest plausible grant, most likely award, and the top three counterarguments

### Highest award a court could plausibly grant

Full treble, 104-week front pay on the FedEx methodology, modest exemplary: wage-loss actuals of $109,989.29 back pay plus approximately $57,200 discounted 104-week front pay ≈ $167,200; statutory additional at 3× ≈ $501,600; subtotal ≈ $668,800; plus interest $14,133; plus exemplary at 1× actuals ≈ $167,200; **approximately $850,100, plus costs and § 1961 post-judgment interest.** The front-pay-to-retirement version of the ask is larger — plug N and r into the formula above and treble the result — but it is the ask, not the plausible grant.

### Most likely award

Back pay cut to the actual judgment date (at $575/week, roughly $72,000–$86,000 if judgment enters in early-to-mid 2027, less any mitigation offset), trebled at the full 3× additional (courts routinely give the ceiling on a willful default), interest recomputed, front pay limited or denied, exemplary denied or replaced by the $25,000 civil penalty for want of a managing-agent and financial-condition record. **Roughly $300,000–$400,000 plus costs**, with the swing factors being the judgment date, mitigation, and the exemplary election.

### Counterargument 1 — Rule 54(c) caps the award at what the prayer fairly noticed, and "according to proof" plus a >$75,000 jurisdictional allegation does not notice $454,000

This is the most dangerous objection, because it does not attack the proof — it attacks the judgment's legal ceiling. **Best response:** the prayer demands the full statutory remedy, and the statute's formula makes the ceiling computable from the complaint itself; the >$75,000 allegation confirms the case was never a small-claims case; and the defendant had the formula, the rate, and the dates in the pleading. **Fallback if the court bites:** ask for the highest amount the court finds noticed, and be ready to show the arithmetic from the complaint's face rather than from extrinsic numbers.

### Counterargument 2 — The April 2028 back-pay period includes wages that have not accrued, and front pay to retirement for an at-will part-time job is speculation, especially with mitigation unaddressed

**Best response:** recharacterize, do not defend the label — post-judgment weeks convert to front pay at the same rate, discounted; the registry-persistence theory plus the job-search log answers speculation; and mitigation is an affirmative defense Home Depot forfeited by defaulting, so the absence of a mitigation record hurts less than it would at trial. **Fallback that keeps most of the money:** the FedEx methodology's 104 weeks of front pay, which a defendant's own removal filing treated as a reasonable horizon.

### Counterargument 3 — Exemplary damages fail § 3294 (no managing agent, no financial-condition evidence, clear-and-convincing standard unmet on a thin admitted record) and double-punish on top of treble damages

**Best response:** elect exemplary under the statute's own authorization, prove the managing-agent/policy link and financial condition with public-record evidence, keep the ask at or below 1× actuals for *Campbell*, and argue treble and exemplary serve different functions — the treble is the Legislature's fixed measure for this violation, the exemplary is the individualized punishment for this defendant's conscious disregard. **Fallback that costs almost nothing if exemplary fails:** elect the $25,000 civil penalty instead, which the statute grants without the § 3294 showing.

## Supporting analogy — and its limit

The FedEx analogy is useful but limited, and the brief should use it only for what it is — a defendant's own *methodology* (hourly rate × hours × weeks, plus 104 weeks of front pay), not an admission about this case. FedEx's removal numbers were amount-in-controversy math expressly assuming arguendo while denying entitlement, against a different defendant. The distress verdicts it cited ($266,000–$625,000) are other cases' outcomes, not a concession. Use the method; do not oversell the source — a court that catches overselling will discount the rest of the brief with it.
