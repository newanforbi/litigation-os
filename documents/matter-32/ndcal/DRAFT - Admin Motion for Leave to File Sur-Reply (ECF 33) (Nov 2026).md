## 2. EXHIBIT A: [PROPOSED] SUR-REPLY

**PLAINTIFF'S [PROPOSED] SUR-REPLY IN OPPOSITION TO DEFENDANTS' MOTION TO DISMISS AND STRIKE (ECF 22)**

### I. The New Door-Entry Immunity Argument Rests on Facts the FAC Does Not Plead.

The reply's immunity question assumes an "unconscious" driver. ECF 33 at 4:28–5:3, 8:5–8. The FAC alleges a fatigued driver who left the travel lanes to rest and was asleep in a normal resting posture in an undamaged car, with no smoke, fire, or debris. FAC ¶¶ 11, 14, 16, 56. On a motion to dismiss, the Court accepts those facts and draws reasonable inferences in Plaintiff's favor. When the parties offer competing plausible explanations, the complaint survives. *Starr v. Baca*, 652 F.3d 1202, 1216 (9th Cir. 2011).

On those facts, the law was clearly established in November 2024:
- Opening a car door and entering the interior is a search that requires probable cause or another particularized justification. *United States v. Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d 1285, 1288–89 (9th Cir. 2020).
- The emergency-aid exception requires "an objectively reasonable basis for believing" that someone inside needs immediate aid. *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 406 (2006); *Hopkins v. Bonvicino*, 573 F.3d 752, 763–64 (9th Cir. 2009).

The reply's effort to distinguish *Hopkins* depends on the officers having "no information." But the FAC alleges what the officers could see. They looked through the windows with flashlights. FAC ¶ 14. They saw an intact car and a person in a resting posture. Id. ¶¶ 11, 16. Sleep in a parked car is not an emergency.

The reply also uses half of Plaintiff's proposed amendment. It cites Plaintiff's statement that he did not respond to "open the window" before the door was opened. ECF 33 at 4:14–16. It omits the rest of that same proposed allegation: Melano spoke to Plaintiff, and he opened the door three seconds later "before Plaintiff could comply." The first welfare question, "Are you okay?", came only after the door was open. ECF [__] at 19:26–20:3 (recording timestamps 00:56:40–00:56:43). An officer who is speaking to an awake driver and receives no sign of distress has no objective basis for emergency aid. Either version of the facts defeats the immunity argument. The argument also comes too late. *Zamani*, 491 F.3d at 997.

### II. *Airs Aromatics* Does Not Bar Correcting Paragraph 16.

An amended pleading may contain allegations that are inconsistent with an earlier pleading. The only bar is a sham, and inconsistency alone does not show one. *PAE Gov't Servs., Inc. v. MPRI, Inc.*, 514 F.3d 856, 858–60 (9th Cir. 2007). *Airs Aromatics* applies that principle. It does not create a rule that a pro se plaintiff may never correct a factual error. The proposed correction is tied to timestamps on the recording that both sides have. It is not a change of theory to escape dismissal. And it makes no difference to the result: on either version, the officers had no objective basis to believe an emergency existed (Part I above).

### III. Leave to Amend Count II Should Be Granted. The Specific Facts Are These.

The reply says Plaintiff identified no specific fabricated statement. ECF 33 at 2:9–13. When deciding whether to grant leave to amend, the Court considers facts raised in opposition papers. *Broam v. Bogan*, 320 F.3d 1023, 1026 n.2 (9th Cir. 2003). The FAC already pleads the facts that an amended Count II would organize around the elements of the claim. A deliberate-fabrication claim requires (1) deliberate fabrication of evidence that (2) caused a deprivation of liberty. *Spencer v. Peters*, 857 F.3d 789, 798 (9th Cir. 2017).

- **Statements.** Meza-Gonzalez's DS-367 and arrest report state that Plaintiff's speech was "slurred," that he "seemed very confused and disoriented," and that there was a "strong distinct odor of an alcoholic beverage." FAC ¶ 26(a)–(c).
- **Contrary record.** The recording captures extended coherent conversation, Plaintiff spelling his surname, discussing the car's mechanics, and correctly identifying his location. It also contains more than two hours of audio in which no officer remarks on any odor. Id. ¶¶ 20, 26(a)–(c).
- **Knowledge.** Meza-Gonzalez was present for everything he described. Id. ¶¶ 40–41, 46. See *Chism v. Washington State*, 661 F.3d 380, 388 (9th Cir. 2011).
- **Deprivation.** The documents were used to obtain the warrant for a compelled blood draw (FAC ¶¶ 29, 36(b)), to support the parole hold that extended Plaintiff's confinement (id. ¶ 36(a)), and as the statutory basis for a one-year administrative suspension of his Class A commercial license (id. ¶¶ 36(d), 37).
- A fabrication claim does not require a criminal charge where the fabrication causes the loss of a protected liberty or property interest. *Costanich v. Dep't of Soc. & Health Servs.*, 627 F.3d 1101, 1108 (9th Cir. 2010).

These allegations are specific. It is far from "absolutely clear" that amendment could not cure any defect. *Akhtar v. Mesa*, 698 F.3d 1202, 1212 (9th Cir. 2012). The conduct underlying Count VI, the refusal of a voluntary PAS test, remains relevant to probable cause under Count III.]

### IV. The Punitive-Damages Prayer Is Not a "Claim" That Can Be Dismissed Under Rule 12(b)(6).

The reply's request to recast the motion to strike as a motion to dismiss the prayer is new, and the Court should decline it. *Zamani*, 491 F.3d at 997. *Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co.*, 618 F.3d 970, 974–75 (9th Cir. 2010), does not permit the same result to be reached under another label. A prayer is a "demand for the relief sought," Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(3), not a claim. Its availability depends on the claims that survive.

On the merits, Rule 9(b) allows malice and other conditions of mind to be alleged generally. Punitive damages under § 1983 require only "reckless or callous indifference" to federally protected rights. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983).

The reply's contention that Melano could not both lack a justification and voice investigative suspicions ("Both cannot be true") is mistaken. Speculating aloud that a cup holds "booze" is not a lawful justification for a search. *Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d at 1288–89. Asking "Are you okay?" after the door was open does not make the opening lawful after the fact.

### V. Conclusion

The Court should disregard the reply's new arguments or reject them for the reasons above. Plaintiff renews the requests stated in his opposition.
