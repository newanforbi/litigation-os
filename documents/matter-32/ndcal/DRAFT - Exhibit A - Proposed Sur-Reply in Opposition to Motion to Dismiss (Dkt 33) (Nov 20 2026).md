### [PROPOSED] SUR-REPLY IN OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS AND MOTION TO STRIKE

Pursuant to leave of Court, Plaintiff Brendan Ngwa Nforbi submits this brief Sur-Reply limited strictly to four new arguments, procedural requests, and record mischaracterizations introduced for the first time in Defendants' Reply Brief (Dkt. 33).

#### I. DEFENDANTS' NEWLY ASSERTED RULE 12(b)(6) STANDARD CONTRADICTS BINDING NINTH CIRCUIT PRECEDENT

Defendants' Reply introduces a novel—and legally incorrect—Rule 12(b)(6) standard. Citing *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007), and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 667 (2009), Defendants tell this Court that "the Court is free to draw all reasonable inferences from the facts alleged, whether such inferences are 'for' or 'against' Plaintiff's theory of liability," and that "where facts within a complaint support both an innocent explanation and an explanation that gives rise to liability, such a complaint ... will not survive a Motion to Dismiss." (Dkt. 33 at 2–3; *see also id.* at 7.)

Ninth Circuit precedent holds the exact opposite on both points:

1. **Inferences run in favor of the plaintiff.** "On a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim, the court must presume all factual allegations of the complaint to be true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party." *Usher v. City of Los Angeles*, 828 F.2d 556, 561 (9th Cir. 1987). The Court may not draw adverse inferences against Plaintiff from unpleaded field sobriety test outcomes or contested police narratives.
2. **Competing plausible explanations survive Rule 12(b)(6).** In *Starr v. Baca*, 652 F.3d 1202 (9th Cir. 2011), the Ninth Circuit squarely rejected the exact reading of *Twombly* and *Iqbal* that Defendants advance here:

> If there are two alternative explanations, one advanced by defendant and the other advanced by plaintiff, both of which are plausible, plaintiff's complaint survives a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6). Plaintiff's complaint may be dismissed only when defendant's plausible alternative explanation is so convincing that plaintiff's explanation is *implausible*.

*Id.* at 1216 (emphasis in original).¹

Defendants' misstated standard drives nearly every section of their Reply—including their claim that Plaintiff's factual allegation of coherent speech on the dashcam video (FAC ¶ 26) may be rejected because coherent speech is "not inconsistent with slurred speech" (Dkt. 33 at 3, 6, 7), and their claim that silence on field sobriety test results supports an adverse inference of intoxication (*id.* at 7). Under *Usher* and *Starr*, those competing factual inferences cannot be resolved against Plaintiff at the pleading stage.

¹ Defendants' repeated citation to page 667 of *Iqbal* (Dkt. 33 at 2, 3, 7) refers to the opinion's factual background, not its pleading analysis (556 U.S. at 678–82). In any event, *Starr* states the binding Ninth Circuit synthesis of *Iqbal*'s "obvious alternative explanation" principle.

#### II. *AIRS AROMATICS* DOES NOT BAR CONFORMING PARAGRAPH 16 TO THE OFFICERS' OWN DASHCAM RECORDING

For the first time on reply, Defendants invoke *Airs Aromatics, LLC v. Victoria's Secret Stores Brand Management, Inc.*, 744 F.3d 595, 600 (9th Cir. 2014), to argue that Plaintiff cannot amend FAC ¶ 16 to align with the CHP dashcam recording—specifically, that the officers' knock woke Plaintiff and that Officer Melano opened the passenger door three seconds after saying "open the window," before Plaintiff had a meaningful opportunity to lower it. (Dkt. 33 at 5, 9.)

Defendants overlook controlling Ninth Circuit precedent establishing that "[t]here is nothing in the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure to prevent a party from filing successive pleadings that make inconsistent or even contradictory allegations." *PAE Gov't Servs., Inc. v. MPRI, Inc.*, 514 F.3d 856, 860 (9th Cir. 2007). In *PAE*, the Ninth Circuit reversed a district court that treated a revised allegation in an amended pleading as an impermissible "sham," explaining that good-faith refinement of facts as a party reviews evidence is a routine part of litigation, not bad faith. *Id.* at 858–60.

*Airs Aromatics* is far afield. There, a counseled trademark plaintiff conceded in its own appellate briefing that its predecessor "was not actively using the marks" for seven years, rendering its legal theory of "continuous usage" impossible as a matter of law. 744 F.3d at 600. Here, by contrast, Plaintiff is a *pro se* litigant seeking to conform a three-word shorthand phrase in FAC ¶ 16 ("while still asleep") to the objective, second-by-second sequence captured on Defendants' own CHP dashcam audio-video recording—the very same recording showing that Officer Melano knocked, instructed Plaintiff to "open the window," and pulled the door open three seconds later without waiting for a response and without first asking a welfare question. Conforming a pleading to an objective recording in Defendants' own possession is candor, not sham pleading, and Defendants cannot simultaneously argue that the knock-and-command sequence defeats Plaintiff's claim (Dkt. 33 at 4) while asserting Plaintiff is barred from pleading that exact sequence accurately.

#### III. PLAINTIFF'S CLARIFICATION OF A CHP FORM FIELD LABEL IS NOT A CONCESSION OF ALCOHOL ODOR

Defendants' Reply twice treats Plaintiff's Opposition as having affirmatively admitted an "odor of alcohol" supporting both probable cause to search and qualified immunity to arrest. (Dkt. 33 at 6 ["Finally, the Opposition newly states that the forms related to the incident refers to 'an odor of an alcoholic beverage, not to a container.' ... Given all the above, Defendants had ample basis for probable cause to search Plaintiff's car."]; *id.* at 8 [building "notices odor of alcohol on the person" into Defendants' qualified-immunity premise].)

That misstates the Opposition and the FAC. Plaintiff's Opposition candidly corrected a wording inaccuracy about what the pre-printed CHP form checkbox *says* (it refers to an odor of an alcoholic beverage rather than an open container). Acknowledging the text printed on an officer's form is not a factual admission that Plaintiff actually emitted an odor of alcohol. To the contrary, FAC ¶ 26 specifically disputes Officer Meza-Gonzalez's intoxication claims and alleges that no alcohol was present, that the cup contained urine rather than alcohol, and that Officer Meza-Gonzalez misrepresented objective observations in seeking a blood-draw warrant. Under *Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.*, 899 F.3d 988, 1003 (9th Cir. 2018), "it is improper to assume the truth of an incorporated document if such assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint." Defendants cannot manufacture an undisputed probable-cause or qualified-immunity predicate out of a disputed officer checkbox merely because Plaintiff accurately quoted the checkbox's title.

#### IV. DEFENDANTS' NEW REQUEST TO CONVERT THEIR RULE 12(f) STRIKE INTO A RULE 12(b)(6) DISMISSAL OF PUNITIVE DAMAGES FAILS PROCEDURALLY AND ON THE MERITS

In their opening brief, Defendants moved to strike punitive damages solely under Rule 12(f). (Dkt. 22 at 14–15.) Confronted with *Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co.*, 618 F.3d 970, 974–75 (9th Cir. 2010)—which holds that Rule 12(f) does not authorize striking damages claims as precluded as a matter of law—Defendants ask for the first time on reply that the Court treat Plaintiff's prayer for punitive damages as "a separate claim within the scope of Defendants' Motion to Dismiss." (Dkt. 33 at 10.)

That belated conversion fails on two independent grounds:

1. **Procedurally improper.** A request raised for the first time in a reply brief may be disregarded. *Zamani v. Carnes*, 491 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2007). Moreover, courts in this District recognize that punitive damages are a *remedy*, not a standalone cause of action, and "a complaint is not subject to a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6) because the prayer seeks relief that is not recoverable as a matter of law." *Saroya v. Univ. of the Pacific*, 503 F. Supp. 3d 986, 1000 (N.D. Cal. 2020).
2. **Merits.** Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages do not require proof of personal hatred or "malice" alone; they are available whenever a defendant's conduct involves "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Defendants' Reply argues only that Officer Melano's post-entry question ("are you okay?") disproves "malice" (Dkt. 33 at 10) and dismisses the judicial-deception allegations against Officer Meza-Gonzalez as "conclusory" (*id.*). Because Count I plausibly alleges deliberate or reckless material falsehoods and omissions in obtaining a blood-draw warrant—and because *Smith v. Wade* expressly holds that reckless indifference suffices even when recklessness is also the underlying liability standard, 461 U.S. at 51–56—Plaintiff's prayer for punitive damages cannot be dismissed on the pleadings.

#### V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and those stated in Plaintiff's Opposition (Dkt. 27), the Court should reject the new arguments and misstated standards in Defendants' Reply (Dkt. 33), deny Defendants' Motion to Dismiss as to Counts I, III, IV, and V and Defendants' Motion to Strike punitive damages, and grant targeted leave to amend as to any defect the Court identifies.
